Ten things examiners look for in Chapter 3

  1. A clear line from each research question to a method. The examiner should be able to point at any RQ and find the data, variables and model that answer it.
  2. Choices that are justified, not just described. “I used stratified sampling” is a description. “I stratified by state because consumption differs sharply between East and West Malaysia” is a justification.
  3. A sampling design you can defend, including the population, sampling frame, selection procedure, sample size and response rate.
  4. A sample size that matches what you want to infer: precision for a proportion, or power for a hypothesis test. A population size alone is not a justification.
  5. Every variable operationalised: the question that measures it, its level of measurement and its coding.
  6. An instrument with a known pedigree: which scales were adopted, which adapted, and the evidence of validity and reliability in your sample.
  7. A model written for your data structure, with the right subscripts (\(i\), \(t\), \(it\)) and every symbol defined.
  8. An estimator chosen for the outcome variable (binary, ordinal, count, continuous) and for the identification problem.
  9. Transparent data handling: what was cleaned, recoded or dropped, and why.
  10. Honest limits: what your design can and cannot support, stated before the results.

A1Methodology, research design and methods

Three words that students use interchangeably and examiners do not. Methodology contains research design, and research design contains methods. Knowing which is which tells you what each part of Chapter 3 has to do.

  • About 20 minutes
  • Worksheet with model answers
  • Five-question quiz

After this section you can:

  1. explain which of methodology, research design and methods is a subset of which;
  2. place any element of your own study at the right level;
  3. write the short philosophy-and-design paragraph that opens an economics Chapter 3.

The short answer

Methodology ⊃ research design ⊃ methods. Methodology is the outermost layer: the logic that says why a certain way of producing knowledge is valid for your problem. Inside it sits the research design, the blueprint for the whole study. Inside the design sit the methods, the specific techniques and tools you use to collect and analyse data.

The three levels in detail

Table A1.1. Methodology, research design and methods compared
LevelQuestion it answersExamples in an economics thesis
MethodologyWhy is this way of knowing valid for my problem?Positivism: relationships between measurable variables can be observed and tested. Phenomenology: understanding lived experience through qualitative data.
Research designWhat is the overall plan, the blueprint?Quantitative cross-sectional survey; panel study of firms; difference-in-differences around a policy change; explanatory sequential mixed methods.
MethodsWhich specific techniques and tools?Stratified random sampling; a 30-item questionnaire; DOSM household survey data; ordered logit; fixed-effects panel regression; Stata 18.

The first lecture's examples of methods are a useful reminder of how broad this layer is. Methods include:

  • the estimation model (OLS, logit, partial proportional odds, DiD, fuzzy RD);
  • the sampling design (simple random, stratified, purposive, snowball);
  • the data collection technique: primary or secondary data; questionnaire survey, interview, focus group discussion (FGD), ethnography, case study; web scraping;
  • the identification strategy (instrumental variables, natural experiment, regression discontinuity);
  • the literature review method (traditional narrative, bibliometric, systematic review, meta-analysis);
  • even the writing method (a monograph or “big book” thesis versus a three-paper thesis) and the referencing format (APA, Harvard, Chicago, MLA).
Examiner: Calling Chapter 3 “Research Methodology” and then describing only methods is common. That is fine, but open with two sentences that place your methods inside a design and a methodology.

Paradigms in one table

A paradigm is a shared set of assumptions about what exists (ontology) and how we can know it (epistemology). Your methodology is the paradigm put to work on your research problem.

Table A1.2. Three paradigms you will meet in economics and the social sciences
ParadigmAssumesTypical designTypical methodsEconomics example
Positivism (post-positivism)An objective reality exists and can be measured, with error.Quantitative: survey, panel, experiment, quasi-experimentProbability sampling, structured questionnaires, secondary data, regressionDoes financial literacy raise household saving?
Interpretivism (including phenomenology)Reality is understood through the meanings people give it.Qualitative: case study, ethnography, phenomenological studyPurposive sampling, in-depth interviews, FGDs, thematic analysisHow do flood victims decide whether to evacuate?
PragmatismUse whatever works to answer the question.Mixed methodsA survey followed by interviews (or the reverse)Survey on e-wallet use, then interviews explaining non-use among B40 households

Most empirical economics is positivist or post-positivist, usually without saying so. You do not need an essay on philosophy. One or two sentences that name the paradigm and connect it to your design are enough. Carter and Little (2007) show how epistemology, methodology and method should be visibly consistent with one another. Morgan (2007) makes the case for pragmatism in applied work.

Examiner: If you name a paradigm, use it. A paragraph on interpretivism followed by a regression model will be queried.

What the Methodology chapter is not

Not a literature review
Prior studies appear here only to justify methodological choices. “Mincer (1974) uses log wages; I follow this convention” belongs in Chapter 3. “Many studies have found positive returns to schooling” belongs in Chapter 2.
Not a results chapter
No estimates, no significance tests, no findings. Those are Chapter 4. Pilot-test reliability statistics are the usual exception.
Not a tutorial
Do not teach your examiner what OLS is. State the model, justify it, and cite a textbook for standard material.
Examiner: In a three-paper thesis each paper carries its own data and methods section. Chapter 3 then becomes a short overview of the common data and design, or disappears entirely. Check your university's rules.

Key takeaways

  1. Methodology contains research design, which contains methods.
  2. Methodology answers why, design answers what plan, methods answer which tools.
  3. In an economics thesis, a short, consistent statement of paradigm and design is better than a long philosophical one.

Worked example: a secondary-data macro study

“Has the inflation–unemployment trade-off in Malaysia changed since 1990?”

  • Methodology: positivist. A stable statistical relationship (a Phillips curve) can be estimated from observed data.
  • Research design: quantitative time-series design with a structural-break analysis.
  • Methods: annual data from DOSM and the World Bank WDI, 1990–2023; ARDL model; Bai–Perron break tests; EViews or Stata.

Notice that the design changes as soon as the question changes. Ask instead how small firms set prices during high inflation and the design becomes qualitative: interviews with owners, purposive sampling, thematic analysis.

A2Correlation, causation and what your design can claim

Every Methodology chapter makes a promise about the kind of answer the study will give. Descriptive, correlational and causal claims need different designs. The threats to a causal reading decide which econometric method you need.

  • About 20 minutes
  • Worksheet with model answers
  • Quiz

After this section you can:

  1. say whether a research question is descriptive, correlational or causal;
  2. name the five classic threats to a causal interpretation and the methods that address each;
  3. write claims in Chapter 3 that match what your design can support.

Three kinds of empirical question

Table A2.1. Descriptive, correlational and causal research
TypeResearch questionMethodsHow the finding is worded
DescriptiveWhat is the pattern?Means, proportions, trends, cross-tabulations“The average wage is higher among university graduates than non-graduates.”
Correlational (association)Are X and Y related, conditional on controls?Correlation; regression with observed controls“Education is positively associated with wages, conditional on age, gender and location.”
CausalDoes changing X cause Y to change?RCT, natural experiment, credible quasi-experimental design with stated identifying assumptions“The programme increased wages by X%, under the identifying assumptions discussed.”

None of these is better than the others. A well-executed descriptive study is worth more than a weak causal one. What matters is that the claim in Chapter 1, the design in Chapter 3 and the wording in Chapter 4 all agree.

Examiner: “Impact”, “effect” and “determinant” are causal words. With a single cross-sectional survey and OLS, write “associated with”.

Why a correlation may not be causal

Table A2.2. Threats to a causal interpretation and the econometric responses
ThreatMeaningExampleEconometric solution
Omitted variable biasAn unobserved factor affects both X and Y.Wages = f(education). Ability affects both education and wages.Fixed effects, instrumental variables, proxy variables
Reverse causalityX affects Y, but Y also affects X.Income = f(training). Higher income may allow more training; more training raises income.Lagged X, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences
Selection biasIndividuals choose treatment based on characteristics related to the outcome.Salary = f(Harvard). Brilliant students choose Harvard and later earn more anyway.Randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental methods
Measurement errorX or Y is measured with noise.Self-reported income is noisy.Instrumental variables, structural equation modelling
SimultaneityX and Y are jointly determined.Price and quantity are set together by supply and demand.Two- and three-stage least squares (2SLS, 3SLS)
Examiner: Name the threat that worries you most and say what your design does about it. Silence reads as unawareness.

Omitted variable bias in one line

Suppose the true model is \(wage_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ_i + \beta_2 ability_i + u_i\), but ability is not in your data. OLS on education alone gives

$$\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta_1 = \beta_1 + \beta_2\,\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(educ, ability)}{\operatorname{Var}(educ)}$$

Ability raises wages (\(\beta_2>0\)) and is positively correlated with education, so \(\hat\beta_1\) overstates the return to schooling. The sign of the bias is the product of the two signs. Being able to state it is exactly the kind of reasoning an examiner wants to see in Chapter 3.

From threat to design

The identification strategy is the part of your methods that turns a correlation into a credible causal estimate. Each standard design rests on one assumption that you must state, and test where possible.

Table A2.3. Identification strategies and their key assumptions
DesignKey assumptionHow you support it in the thesis
Randomised controlled trialRandom assignment balances observed and unobserved characteristics.Balance table of pre-treatment characteristics.
Difference-in-differencesParallel trends: without treatment, both groups would have moved together.Pre-treatment trends graph; event-study coefficients.
Fixed effects (panel)The omitted factor does not change over time.Argue why the confounder is time-invariant; Hausman test against random effects.
Instrumental variablesThe instrument shifts X (relevance) and affects Y only through X (exclusion).First-stage F statistic; argument for exclusion.
Regression discontinuityUnits just either side of the cut-off are comparable.Density test at the cut-off; covariate balance; bandwidth robustness.

Angrist and Pischke (2010) explain why design-based identification now dominates applied economics; Imbens and Wooldridge (2009) survey the estimators and their assumptions. Both are on the reading list.

Key takeaways

  1. Decide in Chapter 3 whether your study is descriptive, correlational or causal, and word every claim accordingly.
  2. Name the main threat to causal interpretation and the method that addresses it.
  3. Every identification strategy rests on an assumption; state it and say how you will check it.

References cited

  • Angrist, J. D., & Pischke, J.-S. (2010). The credibility revolution in empirical economics: How better research design is taking the con out of econometrics. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 24(2), 3–30.
  • Imbens, G. W., & Wooldridge, J. M. (2009). Recent developments in the econometrics of program evaluation. Journal of Economic Literature, 47(1), 5–86.

Worked example: is perceived usefulness a cause of e-wallet use?

The worked survey in this guide (400 students, hypothetical) finds that a one-point higher perceived-usefulness score is associated with a 12-percentage-point higher probability of using an e-wallet (logit average marginal effect 0.119, see B4). Can Chapter 4 say that usefulness causes use?

Reverse causality
Students who already use e-wallets learn how useful they are. Use may raise perceived usefulness rather than the other way round.
Omitted variables
General digital confidence may raise both perceived usefulness and use. It is not measured.
Measurement error
Perceived usefulness is a four-item scale with reliability of about 0.86. Some attenuation is likely (E4).

Verdict: the design supports an association, not a causal effect. Chapter 3 should say so before any results appear. A causal question would need a different design, for example randomly assigning a short demonstration of e-wallet features and comparing later use.

Worked example: an incinerator and house prices

Kiel and McClain (1995) studied house prices near a planned incinerator. Comparing prices near and far from the site in 1981 alone mixes the incinerator's effect with the fact that the area was cheaper all along. The difference-in-differences design compares the change in prices near the site with the change far from it, between 1978 (before the rumour) and 1981 (during construction).

In the simulated version used in C2, the estimated effect is −11,729 (robust SE 3,112), close to the −11,500 built into the simulation. The estimate is causal only if, without the incinerator, prices near and far would have grown at the same rate. That is the parallel-trends assumption, and Chapter 3 must state it.

B13.1 Data: describing where your data come from

The first subsection of Chapter 3 tells the examiner where every number in your thesis comes from. For primary data that means the sampling design, the questionnaire and how its items become your variables. For secondary data it means the datasets, their providers, coverage and versions.

  • About 30 minutes
  • Two model write-ups
  • Template and worksheet

After this section you can:

  1. list everything Section 3.1 must report for primary and for secondary data;
  2. build the table that maps questionnaire items to variables;
  3. write a complete data description an examiner can audit.

What Section 3.1 has to do

Section 3.1 has to let a reader reproduce your dataset in principle: who or what was observed, how they were chosen, how they were measured and what was obtained. Write it so that someone with your instructions could collect the same kind of data again.

If you collected primary data

Report these, in this order. Each item links to the section of this guide that explains it.

  1. Target population and unit of analysis: who the findings are about (for example, undergraduates at public universities in northern Malaysia), and why that population (D1).
  2. Sampling frame: the list you sampled from, how complete it is, and who it misses (D1).
  3. Sampling design: probability or non-probability, the specific design, and why it suits the research questions (D2, D3).
  4. Sample size, justified by precision or by power, and inflated for expected non-response (D4).
  5. The instrument: its sections, number of items, response formats, which scales were adopted or adapted and from whom (E1, E2, E3).
  6. How questionnaire items map to your variables, in a table (below).
  7. Pretesting and pilot: expert review, cognitive interviews, the pilot sample and its reliability results (E4).
  8. Administration: mode (face to face, online, telephone), language versions, fieldwork dates, who collected the data.
  9. Response: number invited, returned and usable, giving the response rate with its denominator.
  10. Ethics: approval reference, consent, anonymity and data protection (or put these in Section 3.5).
Examiner: “The response rate was 80%” means nothing without the numerator and the denominator. Write “400 usable questionnaires from 617 invitations (64.8%)”.

If you use secondary data

  1. The dataset(s): full name, provider, edition or vintage, and date accessed. Data are revised, and results can change with the revision (Johnson et al., 2013).
  2. Coverage: countries, states, households or firms; years; frequency (annual, quarterly, monthly).
  3. Unit of observation and how many observations you use, after merges and exclusions.

One table that pairs every research question with the method that answers it. It is the fastest way to show an examiner that your design is complete, and the fastest way for you to find a research question that has no method.

Why the table matters

Chapter 1 promises research questions; Chapter 4 delivers answers. The RQ–method table in Chapter 3 is the bridge. An examiner reads it first and checks that every question has data, variables and a model, and that each method suits the question it is paired with.

A concept in your theory becomes a variable in your data through operationalisation: a definition, a question or source, a level of measurement and a coding rule. The codebook (or data dictionary) records all of this in one table that anyone can check.

From concept to variable

Operationalisation answers the question: what exactly will I observe, and how will I record it? “Financial literacy” is a concept. “The number of correct answers to three questions on interest, inflation and diversification, scored 0 to 3” is its operationalisation.

For each variable, Section 3.3 should state:

  1. the variable name exactly as it appears in your dataset and tables;
  2. the operational definition, in words;
  3. the questionnaire item or data source it comes from;
  4. the level of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio; see C1);
  5. the coding: values, units, what 1 means for a dummy, how a composite is built;
  6. its role and expected sign, with a source for the expectation where possible.

Equation templates

Copy the form that matches your design, and replace the generic \(x\) with your variables.

Cross-section, continuous outcome (OLS)

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \beta_2 x_{2i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki} + u_i \tag{3.1}$$

Binary outcome (logit)

$$\Pr(y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Lambda(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta) = \dfrac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta)}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta)} \tag{3.2}$$

Ordinal outcome (ordered logit)

$$y_i^* = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta + \varepsilon_i, \qquad y_i = j \text{ if } \kappa_{j-1} < y_i^* \le \kappa_j,\; j = 1,\dots,J \tag{3.3}$$

In the partial proportional odds model (Williams, 2006), some coefficients are allowed to differ across the cut-points \(j\): \(\Pr(y_i > j) = \Lambda(\alpha_j + \mathbf{x}_{1i}'\boldsymbol\beta_1 + \mathbf{x}_{2i}'\boldsymbol\beta_{2j})\).

Nominal outcome (multinomial logit)

$$\Pr(y_i = m \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \dfrac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta_m)}{\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{M}\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta_h)}, \quad \boldsymbol\beta_1 = \mathbf{0} \tag{3.4}$$

Time series (one unit, many periods)

$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_t + \beta_2 y_{t-1} + u_t \tag{3.5}$$

Pooled cross-sections, difference-in-differences

$$y_{it} = \beta_0 + \delta_0\, Post_t + \beta_1\, Treat_i + \delta_1 (Post_t \times Treat_i) + \mathbf{x}_{it}'\boldsymbol\gamma + u_{it} \tag{3.6}$$

Panel, fixed effects

$$y_{it} = \mathbf{x}_{it}'\boldsymbol\beta + a_i + \lambda_t + u_{it} \tag{3.7}$$

Instrumental variables (two-stage least squares)

$$x_i = \pi_0 + \pi_1 z_i + \mathbf{w}_i'\boldsymbol\pi + v_i, \qquad y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \hat x_i + \mathbf{w}_i'\boldsymbol\gamma + u_i \tag{3.8}$$

Regression discontinuity

$$y_i = \alpha + \tau D_i + f(r_i - c) + D_i\, g(r_i - c) + u_i, \quad D_i = \mathbf{1}[r_i \ge c] \tag{3.9}$$

In a fuzzy RD, crossing the cut-off \(c\) raises the probability of treatment without determining it, and \(D_i\) instruments actual treatment, giving a first stage, a reduced form and a second stage (see B2, Example 3).

Examiner: Why is there no error term in Equation (3.4)? Because a probability model has none. Know which of your equations should have one.

Inference: which standard errors?

Heteroskedasticity-robust
The default for cross-sections (vce(robust) in Stata; cov_type="HC1" in Python).
Clustered
When observations are grouped (students in classes, households in villages, years within a state). Cluster at the level of treatment assignment or sampling (vce(cluster id)). With fewer than about 30 clusters, say how you deal with it (for example, a wild cluster bootstrap).
Newey–West (HAC)
For time series with serial correlation (newey).
Survey design
With complex survey data (strata, clusters, weights), use the design (svyset then svy:).

When the method needs a methodological review

A standard estimator needs only a sentence and a textbook citation. A less familiar one needs a short review of the methods literature: what problem the method solves, why the usual alternative fails here, and evidence that the method works. Two examples:

  • Partial proportional odds. The ordered logit assumes each regressor's effect is the same at every cut-point (parallel lines). When a Brant test rejects this for some regressors, Williams (2006) shows how to relax it only for those, keeping the model parsimonious.
  • Semi-nonparametric binary choice. Logit and probit fix the error distribution. Gallant and Nychka (1987) proposed approximating an unknown density with a Hermite series, so the error distribution is estimated rather than assumed. Soon et al. (2018) use this approach for flood evacuation decisions.

  1. Number every equation, define every symbol, and match subscripts to the data structure.
  2. The outcome's measurement level chooses the family of model; the identification problem chooses the design.
  3. State the standard errors and what you will report; review the methods literature for non-standard estimators.

C1Levels of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio

A variable's level of measurement decides which statistics are meaningful and which model suits it as an outcome. Stevens (1946) set out the four levels, often remembered as NOIR.

  • About 15 minutes
  • Sorting game
  • Worksheet with model answers

After this section you can:

  1. assign any variable to its level of measurement;
  2. say which statistics each level permits;
  3. explain what the mean of a dummy variable means.

Nominal

Values are categories with no inherent order. Examples: gender, ethnicity, industry code, country of birth, marital status. The only meaningful comparison is equal or not equal (=, ≠). Permissible statistics: mode, frequencies, proportions, the chi-square test.

Means are meaningless for nominal codes. A “mean gender” is nonsense, even though Stata will obediently compute one if male = 1 and female = 2.

Ordinal

Values have a meaningful order, but the distances between them are not equal, or not known. Examples: income category (B40, M40, T20), a Likert item, academic performance bands (excellent, good, pass). Median and percentiles are meaningful; differences are not. Moving from “agree” to “strongly agree” need not be the same step as from “disagree” to “neutral”.

Interval

Values have order and equal intervals, but no true zero: zero is just a point on the scale, not the absence of the attribute. Examples: temperature in Celsius (0 °C is not “no temperature”), shoe size, calendar year. Differences are meaningful; ratios are not. 20 °C is not “twice as hot” as 10 °C.

Ratio

Interval level plus a true zero that means the absence of the attribute. Examples: income, age, distance, weight, price, GDP, the unemployment rate, number of children, years of schooling. All arithmetic is meaningful: RM 4,000 is twice RM 2,000.

The mean of a dummy variable

If gender is coded as a dummy with female = 0 and male = 1, and Stata reports a mean of 0.57, what does it represent? It is the proportion of observations coded 1: 57% of the sample is male. This is why dummies are coded 0/1 and named after the category coded 1 (male, or better female with female = 1). The mean then reads directly as a share, and the regression coefficient as the difference from the 0 category.

$$\bar{d} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i = \frac{\#\{d_i = 1\}}{n}$$

Key takeaways

  1. Nominal: categories. Ordinal: ordered categories. Interval: equal steps, no true zero. Ratio: equal steps and a true zero.
  2. The level limits the statistics and decides the model for an outcome.
  3. The mean of a 0/1 dummy is the proportion coded 1.

Pooling lets you ask whether relationships changed between periods, and it supports difference-in-differences. Kiel and McClain (1995) studied house prices near a planned incinerator, with houses sold in 1978 (before) and 1981 (after). The hypothesis \(H_0\) is that house prices fall if a house is located near the incinerator, where \(near = 1\) if within 3 km and 0 otherwise.

$$Hprice_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 near_i + u_i \qquad \text{(1981 only: mixes the effect with pre-existing differences)}$$ $$Hprice_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1\, year81_i + \beta_2\, near_i + \beta_3\, (year81_i \cdot near_i) + u_i$$

\(\beta_3\) is the coefficient of interest: the difference-in-differences estimate of the incinerator's effect.

The model carries both subscripts and, usually, unit and time effects:

$$y_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1it} + \dots + \beta_k x_{kit} + a_i + u_{it}$$ $$y_{it} = \beta_1 x_{1it} + \dots + \beta_k x_{kit} + a_i + \lambda_t + u_{it} \qquad \text{(two-way fixed effects)}$$ $$Saving_{it} = \beta_1 FinLit_{it} + \beta_2 Income_{it} + a_i + \lambda_t + u_{it}$$

\(a_i\) captures everything about unit \(i\) that does not change over time, observed or not (geography, institutions, culture). \(\lambda_t\) captures shocks common to all units in year \(t\), such as 2020. Fixed effects remove \(a_i\) by comparing each unit with itself over time; random effects assume \(a_i\) is unrelated to the regressors. The Hausman test helps choose between them.

Protecting data quality in a survey

  • Pretest and pilot before fieldwork: cognitive interviews, then a pilot of 20–40 people outside the sample (E1).
  • Train enumerators and give them a field manual; check a sample of their questionnaires.
  • Build checks into online forms: valid ranges, required answers for key items, skip logic, an attention-check item.
  • Send reminders: two or three reminders typically raise response rates noticeably.
  • Record the paradata: start and finish times, mode, enumerator ID. They help you detect speeders and interviewer effects.

Interviews

Structured
The same questions in the same order; close to a spoken questionnaire.
Semi-structured
A guide of main questions and follow-up probes, with freedom to pursue what the participant raises. The usual choice in economics and business theses.
Unstructured
A conversation around a topic; used in exploratory and ethnographic work.

Kallio et al. (2016) give a five-phase framework for building a semi-structured interview guide. Record interviews (with consent), transcribe them, and analyse them systematically, for example with Braun and Clarke's (2006) thematic analysis. For the number of interviews, justify it by saturation (Guest et al., 2006) or by information power (Malterud et al., 2016), not by a round number.

Focus group discussions

An FGD is a moderated discussion among six to ten people who share a relevant characteristic, such as B40 mothers, SME owners in Kedah or first-time flood victims. The interaction is the point: participants react to each other. Run several groups so you are not relying on one group's dynamics, and keep groups homogeneous on the dimension that matters (Morgan, 1996).

Examiner: Did one FGD of eight people give you “the community's view”? Say how many groups, who was in them, and how you handled dominant speakers.

Key takeaways

  1. Surveys measure and count; interviews and FGDs explain.
  2. The survey mode shapes coverage, response and bias: report it and its consequences.
  3. For qualitative work, justify the number of interviews or groups and describe the analysis method.

Before you commit: six questions about any dataset

  1. Who collected it, and why? An administrative register, a household survey and a model-based estimate have very different error properties.
  2. How was it collected? For surveys: the sampling design, sample size, mode and response rate. Most national household surveys need their weights, and often their strata and clusters, in your estimation.
  3. Does its definition match your concept? “Unemployment” in the WDI is a modelled ILO estimate; in DOSM's Labour Force Survey it is a survey estimate. They can differ.
  4. What is the coverage? Years, countries or states, gaps, and breaks in series when methods change.
  5. Which version? Datasets are revised. Penn World Table results can change between versions (Johnson et al., 2013). Record the version and the date you downloaded it.
  6. Can you use it? The licence, any registration, restricted microdata agreements, and the time they take to approve.
Examiner: Large surveys are not immune to error. Response rates and reporting quality in major household surveys have been falling (Meyer et al., 2015). Say what you know about your dataset's quality.

C5Data cleaning: inspecting, correcting and transforming

Raw data are never ready for analysis. Cleaning means inspecting the data, finding problems, correcting or flagging them by rules you can defend, and creating the variables your models need, all in a script that turns the raw file into the analysis file.

Why clean, and why with a script

Errors in raw data feed straight into your estimates: a keying error of RM 98,000 for a student's monthly allowance shifts the mean and can dominate a regression. A cleaning script (a do-file or notebook) makes every decision visible and repeatable. You can rerun it when you find a new problem, your supervisor can check it, and you can describe it accurately in Chapter 3. Editing cells by hand in Excel leaves no record.

Examiner: Keep the raw file untouched. Save the cleaned data under a new name, and keep the script that links the two (Christensen & Miguel, 2018).

Rules for cleaning decisions

Missing-value codes
Convert codes such as −9 or 999 to true missing before computing anything (mvdecode).
Impossible values
Values outside the allowed range (a 6 on a 1–5 scale; 10 days in a week) are errors. Set them to missing unless the original form shows the true value.
Implausible values
Possible but suspicious (an allowance of RM 98,000). Check the source; if it cannot be verified, set it to missing, winsorise or top-code, and say which. Do not drop real but unusual observations just because they are extreme; robust outlier rules help to flag them (Leys et al., 2013).
Inconsistent answers
Skip-pattern contradictions (a non-user reporting days of use) are resolved by a stated rule, applied to everyone.
Incomplete responses
Decide a threshold in advance, for example dropping respondents who answered fewer than half of the scale items.
Missing data
Report how much is missing for each variable. Listwise deletion is the default; for substantial missingness, consider multiple imputation and explain the assumption (missing at random) it requires (Schafer & Graham, 2002).

Key takeaways

  1. Clean with a script, never by hand; keep raw and clean files separate.
  2. Every change follows a stated rule applied to all observations.
  3. Report the path from raw records to the analysis sample, with numbers.

D1Population, sampling frame and sample

Before choosing a sampling design you must be precise about whom your findings describe, which list you can actually draw from, and the units you select. Most sampling problems that examiners find start here, not in the formula.

  • About 15 minutes
  • Worksheet with model answers

After this section you can:

  1. distinguish population, target population, sampling frame, sampling unit and sample;
  2. identify coverage problems in a sampling frame;
  3. separate sampling error from non-sampling error.

From population to sample

Population
The full set of units the research question concerns.
Target population
The population defined precisely by who, where and when. Your conclusions apply here.
Sampling frame
A list or procedure that identifies the units you can select: enrolment records, the SSM company register, DOSM enumeration blocks, a membership list.
Element (unit of analysis)
The unit you measure and analyse: a student, a household, a firm.
Sampling unit
The unit you select. It can differ from the element: you may select villages, then households within them.
Sample
The units selected. The achieved sample is the subset who actually respond.
Census
Observing every unit in the target population, which is sensible when it is small (for example, every licensed commercial bank).

Frame problems

  • Undercoverage: units missing from the frame. An online panel misses people without internet; a telephone directory misses mobile-only households.
  • Overcoverage: ineligible units on the frame, such as closed firms still registered, or graduates still on an alumni list.
  • Duplicates: units listed twice have twice the chance of selection.
  • Clustering: one frame entry covers several elements, such as one address with several households.
Examiner: “The population is all SMEs in Malaysia.” Which list did you sample from, and who is not on it?

D2Probability sampling: simple random, systematic, stratified

In a probability sample every member of the target population has a known, non-zero chance of selection. That known chance is what allows formal statistical inference: sampling distributions, standard errors and confidence intervals all assume random selection.

  • About 35 minutes
  • Live sampling simulator
  • Allocation calculator
  • Stata and Python code

After this section you can:

  1. explain and carry out simple random, systematic and stratified random sampling;
  2. allocate a stratified sample proportionately or disproportionately, and weight it back;
  3. choose and justify a design for your own study.

1. Simple random sampling (SRS)

Every unit has an equal probability of selection, \(n/N\), and every possible sample of size \(n\) is equally likely. Draw it with a random number generator applied to the sampling frame, or, for a small frame, by drawing paper slips out of a bowl.

  • Needs: a complete list of the population.
  • Strengths: simple; unbiased; the textbook formulas for standard errors apply directly.
  • Weaknesses: small subgroups may be under-represented by chance; can be costly when units are spread out.

2. Systematic random sampling

Select every \(k\)-th unit from the frame after a random start between 1 and \(k\), where \(k = N/n\) (rounded down). With \(N = 2{,}400\) and \(n = 331\), \(k = 7\); a random start of 2 selects units 2, 9, 16, 23 and so on.

  • Strengths: easy in the field, such as every 7th customer leaving a shop or every 10th house on a street; spreads the sample evenly through the list.
  • Weakness: vulnerable to bias when the list has a pattern that matches \(k\), for example if every 10th house on a street is a corner house, or every 7th day is a Sunday.
Examiner: Report the interval and the random start, and say how the frame was ordered.

3. Stratified random sampling

Partition the population into non-overlapping strata (for example urban/rural, ethnic group, industry, faculty) and draw a separate random sample within each. Stratify on characteristics that are related to your outcome, because that is what improves precision.

Proportionate allocation
Each stratum's sample size is proportional to its population size: \(n_h = n \times N_h / N\). Every unit has the same selection probability, so no weights are needed.
Disproportionate allocation
Small strata are over-sampled to improve precision for subgroup estimates. Selection probabilities differ, so population estimates need design weights \(w_h = N_h / n_h\).

D3Non-probability sampling: convenience, purposive, snowball

In non-probability sampling, selection probabilities are unknown, or zero for some units. These designs are used when no sampling frame exists, when the population is hidden, or when the aim is depth rather than statistical generalisation. Findings are hard to generalise, and Chapter 3 must say so honestly.

  • About 20 minutes

1. Convenience sampling

Recruiting whoever is available and willing: students in your class, shoppers at a mall, people who click a link shared on social media.

  • Use it for: pilots, pretests, exploratory studies, testing an instrument.
  • Risk: volunteers and the easily reached differ systematically from the rest. A bigger convenience sample is a bigger biased sample.

2. Purposive (judgemental) sampling

Deliberately selecting units that meet specific criteria, because they hold the information the study needs: female CEOs, judges, central bank economists, SME owners who stopped accepting cash, households flooded three times. Palinkas et al. (2015) describe variants:

  • Expert sampling: people with specialist knowledge.
  • Maximum variation: cases that differ widely, to capture the range of experience.
  • Typical case: cases that represent the usual situation.
  • Critical or extreme case: cases where a phenomenon is most visible.

Purposive sampling is the standard choice for interviews and case studies. State the selection criteria precisely so readers can judge them.

3. Snowball sampling

Existing participants recruit further participants from their networks. It is essential for stigmatised, hidden or illegal populations with no list: undocumented migrant workers, borrowers from unlicensed moneylenders, drug users, the homeless (Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981).

  • Risk: samples reflect the starting “seeds” and their networks; well-connected people are over-represented.
  • Improvement: start from several diverse seeds, limit referrals per person, and record who referred whom. Respondent-driven sampling formalises this to allow some population estimates (Heckathorn, 1997).

4. Quota sampling

Fill quotas that match known population proportions, for example 50% women, or ethnic shares from the census, but select people within each quota by convenience. The sample looks representative on the quota variables, yet selection within quotas is not random. Quota sampling is common in market research and opinion polling.

Is it 30? What the Central Limit Theorem does and does not say

The Central Limit Theorem says that the sampling distribution of a mean approaches a normal distribution as \(n\) grows, whatever the shape of the population. The rule of thumb “\(n \ge 30\)” is a rough guide to when that approximation is good enough for a t-test or a confidence interval on a mean, if the population is not too skewed. For heavily skewed variables such as income, 30 may not be enough even for that.

It says nothing about whether 30 observations are enough to answer your question:

  • Precision: with 30 respondents, a 95% confidence interval for a proportion near 50% is about ±18 percentage points (\(1.96\sqrt{0.25/30} = 0.179\)).
  • Power: with 30 per group, a two-sided t-test has only a 48% chance of detecting a medium effect (\(d = 0.5\)); detecting it with 80% probability needs 64 per group.
  • Regression: with several predictors, 30 observations leave very few degrees of freedom.
Examiner: “The sample exceeds 30, so the CLT applies” justifies nothing about your sample size. Expect to be asked for a calculation.

The Krejcie and Morgan (1970) table

Krejcie and Morgan turned an existing formula for the sample size needed to estimate a population proportion into a lookup table:

$$s = \frac{\chi^2 N P(1-P)}{d^2 (N-1) + \chi^2 P (1-P)}$$
  • \(s\) = required sample size; \(N\) = population size
  • \(\chi^2\) = chi-square value for 1 degree of freedom at the desired confidence level (3.841 at 95%)
  • \(P\) = population proportion, set to 0.50 because \(P(1-P)\) is largest there, giving the most conservative size
  • \(d\) = margin of error as a proportion (0.05)

The table therefore builds in three choices: 95% confidence, ±5 percentage points, and \(P = 0.5\). Its entries agree with the formula to rounding precision. The calculator on the next tab reproduces every entry:

As \(N\) grows, the finite-population correction vanishes and the formula approaches \(n_0 = z^2 P(1-P)/d^2 = 1.96^2 (0.25)/0.05^2 = 384.16\). That is why the required sample barely grows beyond a population of about 10,000. Sampling precision depends mainly on the sample size, not on the population size. Strictly, rounding down does not guarantee the target precision: for \(N = 50{,}000\) a conservative requirement is 382, not 381. This is a minor point.

Five limitations of the table

  1. It assumes probability sampling. The margin-of-error interpretation relies on random selection (usually SRS), a good frame and ignorable non-response. Collect 370 Google Form responses through WhatsApp groups and you have the same number with no comparable inferential justification. Raising a convenience sample from 370 to 1,000 does not remove selection bias (Baker et al., 2013).
  2. It fixes the margin of error at ±5 points. For N = 10,000, ±3 points needs 965 and ±10 points needs 96. Population size alone does not settle the sample size; the precision you need matters far more.
  3. It does not calculate power. The formula is about estimating one proportion. It ignores the effect size a test should detect. A sample of 370 split into two equal groups of 185 has only 48% power to detect a small difference (\(d = 0.2\)), 82% for \(d = 0.3\) and 99.8% for \(d = 0.5\). Detecting \(d = 0.2\) with 80% power needs 394 per group, 788 in total.
  4. It ignores complex designs. Clustered samples carry a design effect \(DEFF = 1 + (m-1)\rho\), with \(m\) respondents per cluster and intraclass correlation \(\rho\). With DEFF = 1.5, 384 clustered respondents are worth about \(384/1.5 = 256\) independent ones.
  5. It gives completed, not invited, responses, and ignores subgroups and regression. To obtain 370 completed questionnaires at a 60% response rate, invite \(370/0.60 \approx 617\). If a key subgroup is 10% of the sample, it has only about 37 members. Regression precision depends on the variance of the regressors, the residual variance, collinearity and the coefficient of interest, none of which the table considers.

Power analysis

Power is the probability of rejecting a false null hypothesis. A priori power analysis fixes four quantities to find the fourth: significance level (usually 0.05), desired power (usually 0.80), the minimum effect size of substantive interest, and the sample size.

  • Regression, all slopes: \(f^2 = 0.15\) with 5 predictors needs N = 92 (Faul et al., 2009; Stata power rsquared gives the same).
  • One coefficient with controls: a small partial effect (\(f^2 = 0.02\)) for one predictor alongside 7 controls needs N = 395. Detecting a small effect of your main variable usually drives the sample size.
  • Logit with a binary outcome: a widely used guideline is at least 10 events (the rarer outcome) per estimated parameter (Peduzzi et al., 1996). With 8 parameters and 25% of respondents in the rarer category, you need at least \(10 \times 8 / 0.25 = 320\) respondents.

Economics has a power problem: Ioannidis et al. (2017) estimate that the median statistical power in many areas of empirical economics is low, which inflates reported effects. A power analysis in Chapter 3 is good practice, not decoration.

Rules of thumb, and their limits

  • Green (1991): N ≥ 50 + 8m to test the overall R², and N ≥ 104 + m for individual predictors (m = number of predictors). Simple, but it ignores effect size; power analysis is better.
  • Five or ten respondents per item for factor analysis: not a guarantee. Required N depends on loadings, number of items per factor and model complexity.
  • Qualitative interviews: justified by saturation (Guest et al., 2006) or information power (Malterud et al., 2016), not by statistical formulas.

E1Designing the questionnaire

The questionnaire is your data-collection instrument. Every variable in your model is only as good as the question that measured it. Good items are clear, neutral, single-barrelled and answerable; good questionnaires flow, protect respondents and are tested before fieldwork.

  • About 30 minutes
  • Fix-the-item exercises
  • Sample questionnaire outline

After this section you can:

  1. distinguish constructs, items and questions, and choose suitable item formats;
  2. spot and rewrite the seven kinds of problem item;
  3. structure, pretest and translate a questionnaire.

Construct, item, question

Instrument
The data-collection tool as a whole: the questionnaire (Q), interview guide or observation sheet.
Construct
The concept you want to measure, often latent: perceived usefulness, financial literacy, trust.
Item
One statement or question with its response options. A construct is usually measured by several items.
Question
Often used loosely for item; strictly, an item phrased as a question (“How many days…?”) rather than a statement.

Item formats

Closed-ended items

  • Dichotomous: yes/no, agree/disagree.
  • Multiple choice: single answer or “select all that apply”. Options must be exhaustive (include “Other, please specify”) and mutually exclusive (income bands must not overlap).
  • Ranking: respondents order alternatives. Beware cognitive load: rank no more than five or six.
  • Likert items: degree of agreement on an ordered scale (E3).
  • Rating scales: satisfaction, importance, frequency, 0–10 (E3).
  • Numeric: age, income, days of use, with the unit and reference period stated.

Open-ended items

Free-text answers give rich information but are hard to code. Use them for (a) pilot studies, to surface categories you missed, and (b) “Other, please specify” options. Numeric answers (age, income) are open-ended too, but easy to analyse.

Items that should not be used

Seven kinds of problem item account for most measurement failures. Try rewriting each before you open the model answer on the Fix the items tab.

Structure and flow

  1. Introduction and consent: who you are, the purpose, how long it takes, anonymity, voluntary participation, contact details.
  2. Screening questions to confirm eligibility (for example, “Are you a full-time undergraduate?”).
  3. Easy, engaging questions first, then the core constructs, grouped by topic with a short heading for each section.
  4. Sensitive questions later (income, debt, illegal behaviour), after trust is built.
  5. Demographics last, except those needed for screening or skip logic.
  6. Close with thanks and, if appropriate, an open comments box.

Keep response options in a consistent direction within a block (for example, 1 = strongly disagree on the left), state reference periods (“in the past 7 days”), and keep the questionnaire short. Every item should map to a variable in your codebook (B3). If it doesn't, delete it.

Testing before fieldwork

Expert review
Two to five experts judge relevance and clarity, which supports content validity (E4).
Cognitive interviews
A handful of target respondents think aloud while answering, revealing how they understand each question (Beatty & Willis, 2007).
Pilot
Usually 20–40 respondents outside the main sample: checks timing, skip logic, coding and preliminary reliability. If items change substantially, test them again.
Translation
For English–Malay (or other) versions: forward translation, independent back-translation, and reconciliation of differences (Brislin, 1970; Beaton et al., 2000).

Key takeaways

  1. One idea per item, in neutral, specific, everyday words, with a stated reference period.
  2. Order: consent, screening, easy, core, sensitive, demographics.
  3. Expert review, cognitive interviews, pilot and back-translation before fieldwork.

E2Adopting and adapting established questionnaires

You rarely need to write every item from scratch. Established instruments come with evidence of validity and reliability, and they let you compare your results with earlier studies. Using them well means choosing carefully, changing as little as possible, documenting every change, and testing again in your own sample.

  • About 25 minutes
  • Adaptation log template
  • Worked example from TAM

After this section you can:

  1. distinguish adopting, adapting and developing an instrument;
  2. evaluate an existing instrument's fit for your study;
  3. document adaptations so an examiner can see exactly what changed and why.

Adopt, adapt or develop?

Adopt
Use the instrument as published: same items, same response format, same scoring. Its validity evidence carries over most directly, although you still check reliability in your sample.
Adapt
Modify it: change the context words, tense or target behaviour; translate it; drop or add items; change the response format. Every change weakens the inherited evidence, so validate again.
Develop
Write new items when no suitable instrument exists. This needs the full scale-development process: item generation, expert review, pretesting and factor analysis (Hinkin, 1998; Boateng et al., 2018).

Start from the research questions and find an established instrument for each construct, recording its source, length and response format. Here is an example from a study of a teacher preparation programme and co-teaching:

A procedure for adopting and adapting

  1. Define the construct precisely before searching. “Financial literacy” as objective knowledge needs a test; as confidence it needs attitude items.
  2. Search the literature on your construct: the original development papers, review articles, highly cited applications, measurement databases.
  3. Evaluate each candidate: does its definition match yours? What validity and reliability evidence exists, and in which populations? What length and format? Is it free to use?
  4. Get permission where needed. Some instruments are copyrighted or sold under licence; write to the authors or publisher and keep their reply for your appendix.
  5. Adapt minimally and systematically: change only what the new context requires, keep the response format, and record every change in an adaptation log.
  6. Translate properly: forward translation, independent back-translation, reconciliation (Brislin, 1970; Beaton et al., 2000).
  7. Review and pretest: expert content review (content validity index), cognitive interviews, pilot (E1, E4).
  8. Re-establish evidence in your sample: reliability (alpha, omega), factor structure, convergent and discriminant validity. A scale's reliability in someone else's sample is not evidence about yours.
  9. Report: a sources table in Section 3.1 and the full adaptation log in an appendix.
Examiner: “The questionnaire is reliable because it was adopted from a published article.” Reliability belongs to scores in a sample, not to a printed questionnaire. Show me yours.

Key takeaways

  1. Prefer established instruments; adopt where you can, adapt where you must, develop only when nothing fits.
  2. Change as little as possible, keep the response format, and log every change.
  3. Re-establish reliability and validity in your own sample.

E3Likert scales and rating scales

The first misconception to correct: Likert scales and rating scales are not two mutually exclusive methods. A Likert-type item is one particular kind of rating scale. What matters is what you intend to measure, how respondents answer, and which analyses the answers can legitimately support.

  • Format examples

  1. distinguish a Likert-type item, a Likert scale and other rating scales;
  2. choose a response format to match each construct;
  3. choose an estimator for an outcome measured on these scales.

What is the difference?

A rating scale is the broad category: respondents evaluate something using ordered categories or numerical scores. A Likert-type item is a more specific format that asks for degree of agreement or disagreement with a statement. Strictly, a Likert scale is several related Likert-type items combined (summed or averaged) to measure one underlying construct (Likert, 1932). A single agreement question is a Likert-type item, although many researchers loosely call it a Likert scale.

Example A: Likert-type item

“Using ChatGPT improves my academic productivity.”

Measures agreement with a proposition.

Example B: numerical rating scale

“How satisfied are you with your university's Wi-Fi service?”

Very dissatisfied 7/10 Very satisfied

Directly evaluates satisfaction.

Both collect subjective evaluations, but the first operationalises agreement with a statement, while the second rates the thing itself.

Agreement items for latent constructs. To measure perceived usefulness of AI, use several statements on the same five-point agreement format:

$$PU_i = \frac{PU1_i + PU2_i + PU3_i + PU4_i}{4}$$

Agreement formats also invite acquiescence bias, the tendency to agree whatever the content. An experiment by Revilla, Saris and Krosnick (2014) found that five categories performed better than seven or eleven for the agree–disagree scales they examined. That is evidence for that context, not a universal rule.

Is OLS acceptable for Likert data? The debate is long-running. Individual items are ordinal: moving from 1 to 2 need not equal moving from 4 to 5 (Jamieson, 2004). E4Validity and reliability

Validity asks whether you are measuring the right thing, and whether your interpretations are justified. Reliability asks whether you are measuring it consistently. A measure can be reliable without being valid, so both have to be established, and for your questionnaire they are established with evidence from your own data.

What validity and reliability mean

Validity
Are we measuring what we intend to measure, and are the interpretations and conclusions we draw justified? Modern measurement theory treats validity as the degree to which evidence and theory support particular interpretations of scores for particular purposes (Messick, 1995). So the rigorous question is not “is this questionnaire valid?” but “are these scores valid for this interpretation, in this population, for this purpose?”
Reliability
How consistent, stable or precise are the measurements under conditions where they should agree?

The same idea with numbers: an object truly weighs 70 kg, and four scales each weigh it four times.

Random error (distraction, inconsistent interpretation, changing conditions) makes measurements fluctuate and mainly threatens reliability. Systematic error (consistently overstating income; a miscalibrated instrument; items that capture a different concept) pushes measurements one way and mainly threatens validity, possibly alongside high reliability. Both can happen at once.

A study can have excellent measurement validity and reliability yet poor internal validity, if its identification strategy is weak. Similarly, measurement reliability is not the same as reproducibility (getting the same results from the same data and code) or replicability (finding the same result in new data). In qualitative research, quality is usually judged through trustworthiness: credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability.

The traditional classification (adapted from Sekaran and Bougie, 2016, Figure 12.1) splits the “goodness” of measures into reliability and validity. Select any box to see its meaning, an economics example, how to assess it, and a guideline.

Goodness of data

Before data are analysed, the measures that produced them must be shown to be good: reliable (consistent) and valid (measuring the intended concept). Reliability is necessary for validity but not sufficient.

Select a box on the left for detail.

Reliability

Question: do the measurements agree with themselves, over time (stability) and across items (consistency)?

A reliable measure of perceived financial insecurity gives similar scores when nothing has changed, and its items move together.

Reliability is a property of scores in a sample, not of a printed questionnaire. Report it for your data.

Stability

Question: does the measure give the same result when the construct has not changed?

Assessed by repeating the measurement (test–retest) or by using equivalent versions (parallel forms). Only meaningful for constructs expected to be stable over the interval.

Test–retest reliability

Meaning: the same respondents complete the same instrument twice, under comparable conditions.

Economics example: 50 students take a financial-knowledge test and retake it two weeks later, with no teaching in between. High agreement is evidence of temporal stability.

How to assess: intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous scores (choose the form carefully: Shrout & Fleiss, 1979); weighted kappa for categories. Pearson's r measures association and can stay high even when everyone scores higher the second time.

Guideline: ICC below 0.50 poor, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, above 0.90 excellent (Koo & Li, 2016).

Choose the interval with care: too short and people remember answers; too long and the construct may genuinely change. Financial anxiety may change after a scholarship or a job loss, and that is real change, not unreliability.

Parallel-form reliability

Meaning: two equivalent versions of an instrument give comparable scores.

Economics example: two versions of a financial-literacy quiz with different numbers in the compound-interest and inflation questions, so that a pre-test and post-test around a workshop do not reward memory.

How to assess: correlation, or agreement, between scores on the two forms in the same respondents.

Equivalence is hard to achieve. Check that the forms have similar means and variances.

Consistency

Question: do the items meant to measure one construct hang together?

Assessed by inter-item consistency (Cronbach's alpha, McDonald's omega) and by split-half reliability. Internal consistency is not the same as stability over time.

Inter-item consistency reliability

Meaning: respondents answer the items of one scale consistently, because the items reflect the same construct.

Economics example: the four perceived-usefulness items in the e-wallet survey give α = 0.86 (worked example).

How to assess: Cronbach's alpha (Cronbach, 1951), \(\alpha = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{\sum \sigma_i^2}{\sigma_T^2}\right)\); McDonald's omega from a factor model, preferable when loadings differ (McNeish, 2018).

Guideline: 0.70 is the usual minimum for research use, 0.80+ good; above 0.95 may mean redundant items. These are conventions, not laws.

Alpha rises with the number of items even when items do not improve; it does not show the scale measures one dimension; and it is not evidence of validity (Sijtsma, 2009). Compute it per scale, never across the whole questionnaire.

Split-half reliability

Meaning: scores on two halves of a scale (for example odd and even items) agree.

Example: a ten-question knowledge test split into odd and even questions, with r = 0.70 between the halves.

How to assess: correlate the halves, then correct for halving the length with the Spearman–Brown formula, \(r_{SB} = \frac{2r}{1+r}\): here \(2(0.70)/1.70 = 0.82\).

The result depends on how you split. Alpha is the average of all possible split-half coefficients, which is one reason it is reported more often.

Validity

Question: are we measuring the right thing?

Traditional textbooks distinguish content, criterion-related and construct validity. Modern theory treats these as different sources of evidence for one validity argument (the AERA, APA and NCME Standards; Messick, 1995). See the five sources of evidence below the tree.

Logical (content) validity

Meaning: the items cover the relevant dimensions of the construct, and only those.

Economics example: financial literacy defined as knowledge of interest, inflation, diversification and debt. A questionnaire that asks only about interest omits half the domain (construct under-representation), however well its items are written.

How to assess: a panel of experts rates each item's relevance on a 4-point scale. Item-level CVI = share of experts rating it 3 or 4; scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) = mean of the I-CVIs. Lawshe's (1975) content validity ratio is an alternative.

Guideline: I-CVI ≥ 0.78 with six or more experts; S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 (Polit et al., 2007). Use the CVI calculator in the Calculators tab.

Expert agreement supports item content; it does not show respondents understand the items, or that the expected structure holds in the data.

Face validity

Meaning: the instrument looks as if it measures the construct, to respondents and lay reviewers.

Economics example: “Using an e-wallet makes my payments more efficient” looks like a usefulness item; “I enjoy shopping with my friends” does not.

How to assess: ask a few target respondents and reviewers whether items seem understandable, relevant and appropriate; cognitive interviews go further.

The weakest form of evidence: necessary for cooperation, never sufficient on its own.

Criterion-related validity

Question: do scores relate as expected to an independently measured outcome (the criterion)?

Split into concurrent (criterion measured at the same time) and predictive (criterion measured later). The evidence is only as good as the criterion: monthly spending is not a valid benchmark for financial literacy, because spending also reflects income.

Predictive validity

Meaning: scores predict a relevant future outcome.

Economics example: an intention-to-use scale collected in March predicts the number of e-wallet transactions recorded over the following three months.

How to assess: correlation or regression of the later criterion on the score; for a binary criterion, classification accuracy or AUC.

Prediction is not causation, and attrition between waves can bias the estimate.

Concurrent validity

Meaning: scores relate to a criterion measured at about the same time, or distinguish groups known to differ.

Economics example: self-reported e-wallet use compared with the same month's transaction records; a financial-knowledge test on which finance graduates outscore other graduates (known-groups comparison).

How to assess: correlation with the criterion; difference in means between known groups.

Congruent (construct) validity

Meaning: the instrument measures the theoretical construct it claims to, especially a latent one such as trust, confidence or perceived security (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955).

How to assess: factor analysis (EFA when the structure is uncertain, CFA to test a hypothesised structure), plus convergent and discriminant evidence.

Passing KMO and Bartlett's tests shows only that the data can be factor-analysed, not that the construct is valid.

Convergent validity

Meaning: measures that should be related are related: a scale's items load strongly on their factor, and the score correlates with other measures of the same construct.

Economics example: students who score high on perceived e-wallet security also score high on an established scale of trust in digital-payment security.

How to assess: standardised loadings; average variance extracted, \(AVE = \overline{\lambda^2}\); composite reliability, \(CR = (\sum\lambda)^2 / [(\sum\lambda)^2 + \sum(1-\lambda^2)]\) (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

Guideline: loadings ≥ 0.70, AVE ≥ 0.50, CR ≥ 0.70, as rules of thumb, not laws. CR is really a reliability coefficient.

Discriminant validity

Meaning: measures of constructs that should differ do differ (Campbell & Fiske, 1959).

Economics example: perceived usefulness (“e-wallets help me pay efficiently”) and ease of use (“learning to use an e-wallet is easy”) are related but distinct; their scales should not collapse into one factor.

How to assess: Fornell–Larcker criterion (√AVE of each construct exceeds its correlations with other constructs); heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT), which detects problems the Fornell–Larcker criterion misses (Henseler et al., 2015); cross-loadings.

Guideline: HTMT below 0.85 (strict) or 0.90 (lenient).

Why reliability matters for your regression

If the true model is \(Y_i = \alpha + \beta X_i^* + \varepsilon_i\) but you observe \(X_i = X_i^* + u_i\) with classical measurement error, OLS converges to

$$\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta = \beta\,\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X^*)}{\operatorname{Var}(X^*) + \operatorname{Var}(u)}$$

The fraction is the reliability ratio. With reliability 0.70, a true coefficient of 0.50 is estimated near 0.35: attenuation bias. The result holds for classical error in a regressor; non-classical errors can bias in other directions, and error in the dependent variable has different consequences. Measurement reliability is not just a psychometric nicety; it changes your coefficients (Bound & Krueger, 1991, show how large reporting error is in survey earnings).

Key takeaways

  1. Reliability is consistency; validity is meaning. Reliable does not mean valid.
  2. Collect several kinds of validity evidence (content, response process, structure, relations to other variables) and report reliability per scale, from your own data.
  3. Report alpha and omega/CR, AVE and HTMT with their limits; never let one coefficient stand in for validity.

The concept to be measured, often latent, such as trust or perceived usefulness (E1).
Content validity index (CVI)
The proportion of experts rating an item relevant; averaged across items for a scale-level index (E4).
Convenience sampling
Recruiting whoever is available and willing (D3).
Cronbach's alpha
A coefficient of internal-consistency reliability for a set of items (E4).
Cross-sectional data
Many units observed at one point in time (C2).

D

Design effect (DEFF)
The ratio of a complex design's variance to that of a simple random sample of the same size; \(1 + (m-1)\rho\) for equal clusters (D4).
Design weight
The inverse of a unit's selection probability, \(N_h/n_h\) in stratified sampling (D2).
Difference-in-differences (DiD)
Compares the change in outcomes for a treated group with the change for a comparison group (C2, B4).
Discriminant validity
Evidence that measures of different constructs are distinct (Fornell–Larcker, HTMT) (E4).
Dummy variable
A 0/1 variable named after the category coded 1; its mean is the share coded 1 (C1).

E

Effect size
The magnitude of a difference or association, such as Cohen's d or f², used in power analysis (D4).
Exclusion restriction
The assumption that an instrument affects the outcome only through the endogenous regressor (A2).
External validity
Whether findings apply beyond the study's setting and sample (E4).

F

Fixed effects
Unit-specific intercepts in panel data that absorb all time-invariant characteristics (C2).
Focus group discussion (FGD)
A moderated group discussion among 6–10 people sharing a relevant characteristic (C3).
Formative index
A composite whose indicators define the construct and need not correlate; alpha is inappropriate (E4).
Fornell–Larcker criterion
Discriminant validity holds if each construct's √AVE exceeds its correlations with other constructs (E4).

H

Hausman test
Tests whether fixed- and random-effects estimates differ systematically; rejection favours fixed effects (C2).
HTMT
Heterotrait–monotrait ratio of correlations; below 0.85 (or 0.90) supports discriminant validity (E4).

I

Identification strategy
The design feature that lets an estimate be read causally, together with its assumption (A2).
Instrument (data collection)
The tool used to collect data: questionnaire, interview guide, observation sheet (E1).
Internal validity
Whether an estimated relationship can credibly be read as causal (A2, E4).
Interval scale
Ordered values with equal intervals but no true zero (C1).

L

Likert scale
Strictly, several agreement items combined to measure one construct; a single item is a Likert-type item (E3).

M

Margin of error
Half the width of a confidence interval; ±5 percentage points in the Krejcie–Morgan table (D4).
Methodology
The philosophy and logic that justify how knowledge is produced; it contains research design, which contains methods (A1).
Methods
The specific techniques and tools of data collection and analysis (A1).

N

Nominal scale
Categories without order (C1).
Non-probability sampling
Designs in which selection probabilities are unknown or zero for some units (D3).

O

Omega (McDonald's ω)
A factor-model-based reliability coefficient that allows unequal loadings (E4).
Omitted variable bias
Bias from leaving out a variable that affects the outcome and is correlated with an included regressor (A2).
Operationalisation
Turning a concept into a measurable, coded variable (B3).
Ordinal scale
Ordered categories with unknown or unequal intervals (C1).

P

Panel data
The same units observed repeatedly over time (C2).
Paradigm
A set of assumptions about what exists and how it can be known: positivism, interpretivism, pragmatism (A1).
Parallel trends
The DiD assumption that treated and comparison groups would have moved together without treatment (A2).
Pilot study
A small trial of the instrument and procedures before fieldwork (E1).
Pooled cross-sections
Independent samples from the same population at different times (C2).
Power
The probability of detecting an effect of a given size when it exists; usually set at 0.80 (D4).
Probability sampling
Every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection (D2).
Purposive sampling
Deliberately selecting units that meet criteria relevant to the research (D3).

R

Ratio scale
Equal intervals and a true zero (C1).
Reflective measurement
A model in which the latent construct causes its indicators, which should therefore correlate (E4).
Reliability
The consistency or stability of measurement (E4).
Research design
The overall plan of a study; sits between methodology and methods (A1).
Response rate
Usable responses divided by eligible units invited (B1).
RQ–method table
A table pairing each research question with its data, variables and method (B2).

S

Sampling frame
The list or procedure from which the sample is drawn (D1).
Saturation
The point at which new interviews add no new themes (C3).
Simple random sampling
Every unit, and every sample of size n, has an equal chance of selection (D2).
Snowball sampling
Participants recruit further participants; used for hidden populations (D3).
Stratified random sampling
Random sampling within non-overlapping subgroups (strata) (D2).
Systematic sampling
Selecting every k-th unit after a random start (D2).

T

Target population
The precisely defined population the findings describe (D1).
Test–retest reliability
Agreement between scores from two administrations of the same instrument (E4).
Time-series data
One unit observed over many periods (C2).

V

Validity
The degree to which evidence supports the intended interpretation of scores, and more broadly the soundness of conclusions (E4).

Running the code

Stata
Unzip, open Stata, change the working directory to downloads/code (File → Change Working Directory), then run the do-files in order: do 03_data_cleaning.do first, because 04 and 06 use its output. Built-in commands only; Stata 16 or later.
Python
Open the notebooks in Jupyter, VS Code or Google Colab. The first cell installs any missing package. Run 03_data_cleaning.ipynb before 04 and 06. On Colab the data load from this guide's GitHub repository automatically.
Tested
Every do-file and notebook is run automatically each time this guide is rebuilt, and must finish without a single error before the ZIP is produced.

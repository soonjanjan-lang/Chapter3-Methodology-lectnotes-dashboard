B13.1 Data: describing where your data come from

The first subsection of Chapter 3 tells the examiner where every number in your thesis comes from. For primary data that means the sampling design, the questionnaire and how its items become your variables. For secondary data it means the datasets, their providers, coverage and versions.

  • About 30 minutes
  • Two model write-ups
  • Template and worksheet

After this section you can:

  1. list everything Section 3.1 must report for primary and for secondary data;
  2. build the table that maps questionnaire items to variables;
  3. write a complete data description an examiner can audit.

What Section 3.1 has to do

Section 3.1 has to let a reader reproduce your dataset in principle: who or what was observed, how they were chosen, how they were measured and what was obtained. Write it so that someone with your instructions could collect the same kind of data again.

If you collected primary data

Report these, in this order. Each item links to the section of this guide that explains it.

  1. Target population and unit of analysis: who the findings are about (for example, undergraduates at public universities in northern Malaysia), and why that population (D1).
  2. Sampling frame: the list you sampled from, how complete it is, and who it misses (D1).
  3. Sampling design: probability or non-probability, the specific design, and why it suits the research questions (D2, D3).
  4. Sample size, justified by precision or by power, and inflated for expected non-response (D4).
  5. The instrument: its sections, number of items, response formats, which scales were adopted or adapted and from whom (E1, E2, E3).
  6. How questionnaire items map to your variables, in a table (below).
  7. Pretesting and pilot: expert review, cognitive interviews, the pilot sample and its reliability results (E4).
  8. Administration: mode (face to face, online, telephone), language versions, fieldwork dates, who collected the data.
  9. Response: number invited, returned and usable, giving the response rate with its denominator.
  10. Ethics: approval reference, consent, anonymity and data protection (or put these in Section 3.5).
Examiner: “The response rate was 80%” means nothing without the numerator and the denominator. Write “400 usable questionnaires from 617 invitations (64.8%)”.

If you use secondary data

  1. The dataset(s): full name, provider, edition or vintage, and date accessed. Data are revised, and results can change with the revision (Johnson et al., 2013).
  2. Coverage: countries, states, households or firms; years; frequency (annual, quarterly, monthly).
  3. Unit of observation and how many observations you use, after merges and exclusions.
  4. A source table: one row per variable, giving definition, series code, source and any transformation.
  5. How the provider collected the data, briefly: for a survey such as DOSM's Household Income and Expenditure Survey, the sampling design and sample size; for administrative data, how records are generated.
  6. Known limitations: breaks in series, changes in definitions, missing years, top-coding.
Examiner: Give the series code (for example, World Bank FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG) so a reader can download exactly what you used.

Key takeaways

  1. Section 3.1 should let a reader reproduce your data in principle.
  2. For primary data, report population, frame, design, size, instrument, item map, pilot, administration and response, with numbers.
  3. For secondary data, report the dataset, provider, vintage, coverage, unit, series codes and limitations.

References cited

  • Davis, F. D. (1989). Perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and user acceptance of information technology. MIS Quarterly, 13(3), 319–340.
  • Johnson, S., Larson, W., Papageorgiou, C., & Subramanian, A. (2013). Is newer better? Penn World Table revisions and their impact on growth estimates. Journal of Monetary Economics, 60(2), 255–274.
  • Lusardi, A., & Mitchell, O. S. (2014). The economic importance of financial literacy: Theory and evidence. Journal of Economic Literature, 52(1), 5–44.

The running example in this guide is a hypothetical survey: “Determinants of e-wallet use among undergraduates at a public university in northern Malaysia.” Here are the design decisions that Section 3.1 has to report, with the numbers.

Worksheet B1

  1. A draft says: “Data were collected from 300 respondents using a questionnaire. The response rate was good.” List five pieces of information missing from it. (5 marks)

    Any five of: the target population; the sampling frame; the sampling design and its justification; how the sample size was determined; the number invited (so the response rate can be computed); the questionnaire's structure and the sources of its scales; the pilot; the mode and dates of administration; exclusions from returned to usable; ethics approval.

  2. Build the item-to-variable map (Section, Items, Variable, Format, Source) for a study of household saving with three constructs: saving behaviour (3 items), financial literacy (5 questions), and present bias (4 agreement items adapted from a published scale). (6 marks)

    SectionItemsVariableFormatSource
    BB1–B3Saving behaviour (saves monthly; amount; saving rate)Yes/No; RM; %Researcher-developed
    CC1–C5Financial literacy score, 0–5Multiple choice, scored correct/incorrectName the published questions used
    DD1–D4Present bias (mean of four items)5-point agreementAdapted from the named published scale

    Marks for: one row per construct, item numbers, measurement format, and a source for every adopted or adapted scale.

  3. You use DOSM's Household Income and Expenditure Survey microdata. What should Section 3.1 say about how DOSM collected the data, and why? (4 marks)

    Briefly: the survey year, its sampling design (a stratified multistage household sample) and achieved sample size, the mode of interview, and the reference period for income and expenditure. Cite the official methodology report. This tells the examiner how representative your data are and whether you need to use the survey weights and design variables in estimation.

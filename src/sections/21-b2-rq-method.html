B23.2 The RQ–method table

One table that pairs every research question with the method that answers it. It is the fastest way to show an examiner that your design is complete, and the fastest way for you to find a research question that has no method.

  • About 20 minutes
  • Four real examples
  • Template and worksheet

After this section you can:

  1. build an RQ–method table with the right columns for your study;
  2. match each question to a method suited to its outcome and data structure;
  3. spot a question the design cannot answer before the examiner does.

Why the table matters

Chapter 1 promises research questions; Chapter 4 delivers answers. The RQ–method table in Chapter 3 is the bridge. An examiner reads it first and checks that every question has data, variables and a model, and that each method suits the question it is paired with.

Which columns to include

Table B2.1. Columns for an RQ–method table
ColumnWhen to include it
Research question (and sub-questions)Always. Use exactly the numbering and wording of Chapter 1.
Method or modelAlways. Name the estimator (“ordered logit”), not just “regression”.
Outcome variable(s)Recommended. The outcome's measurement level decides the model.
Main explanatory variable(s)Recommended.
Data source and unitWhen different questions use different data or units.
HypothesisWhen the thesis is hypothesis-testing.
Data collection method; theoryOptional. Useful in mixed-methods designs.
Section where answeredOptional. A pointer to the Chapter 4 section helps long theses.

Rules for a good table

  1. One row per question or sub-question. If a row needs two unrelated methods, the question probably needs splitting.
  2. The method must fit the outcome. “What are their intentions?” needs summary statistics. “How intense are their intentions?” on an ordered scale needs an ordinal model. “Did the policy change outcomes?” needs a design such as DiD (see B4).
  3. The method must fit the data structure. A panel question needs a panel estimator; a single cross-section cannot answer a question about change over time (C2).
  4. Descriptive questions get descriptive methods. Not every row needs a regression.
  5. Wording must match Chapter 1 exactly. Examiners notice when RQ2 has quietly changed.
Examiner: If a method appears in 3.4 but in no row of this table, I will ask which question it answers.

Key takeaways

  1. Every research question and sub-question gets a row and a named method.
  2. The outcome's measurement level and the data structure decide the method.
  3. Keep RQ wording identical to Chapter 1, and make sure every model in 3.4 appears in the table.

Worksheet B2

  1. For each question, name a suitable method and the measurement level of the outcome: (a) What proportion of B40 households have an emergency fund? (b) What factors are associated with the number of children a household has? (c) Which characteristics distinguish households that save in ASB, in a bank account, or not at all? (d) Did the 2022 increase in the minimum wage change working hours? (8 marks)

    (a) Binary outcome; proportion with a confidence interval, using survey weights. (b) Count outcome (ratio level, non-negative integers); Poisson or negative binomial regression. (c) Nominal outcome with three unordered categories; multinomial logit. (d) Ratio-level outcome, causal policy question; difference-in-differences comparing affected and less-affected workers before and after 2022, or a panel with individual fixed effects.

  2. What is wrong with this row? “RQ2: How has household debt changed between 2016 and 2022? | Method: OLS regression | Data: 2022 household survey.” (4 marks)

    The question is about change over time, but the data are a single cross-section from 2022, so they cannot show change. The design needs at least two time points: pooled cross-sections from both survey years (comparing distributions or estimating with a year dummy) or a panel. “OLS regression” is also too vague; the table should name the outcome and the specification.

  3. Draft a three-row RQ–method table for your own thesis (or for the topic “remittances and household education spending in Malaysia”). (6 marks)

    A possible answer: RQ1 “What share of households receive remittances, and how much?” uses a household survey with proportions and means. RQ2 “Are remittances associated with higher education spending?” takes the education budget share as the outcome (ratio level), remittance receipt as the main explanatory variable, and OLS with controls; a fractional response model if many zeros. RQ3 “Does the association differ for daughters and sons?” adds an interaction of remittances and the share of daughters. Marks for wording, levels and named estimators.

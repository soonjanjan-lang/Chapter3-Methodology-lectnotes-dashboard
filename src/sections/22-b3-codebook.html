B33.3 Variables: operationalisation and the codebook

A concept in your theory becomes a variable in your data through operationalisation: a definition, a question or source, a level of measurement and a coding rule. The codebook (or data dictionary) records all of this in one table that anyone can check.

  • About 25 minutes
  • Published example plus three more
  • Stata and Python code

After this section you can:

  1. operationalise a concept into a measurable, coded variable;
  2. build a variable operationalisation table for Section 3.3;
  3. name, label and code variables so the data and the thesis agree.

From concept to variable

Operationalisation answers the question: what exactly will I observe, and how will I record it? “Financial literacy” is a concept. “The number of correct answers to three questions on interest, inflation and diversification, scored 0 to 3” is its operationalisation.

For each variable, Section 3.3 should state:

  1. the variable name exactly as it appears in your dataset and tables;
  2. the operational definition, in words;
  3. the questionnaire item or data source it comes from;
  4. the level of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio; see C1);
  5. the coding: values, units, what 1 means for a dummy, how a composite is built;
  6. its role and expected sign, with a source for the expectation where possible.

A published example

Notice three good habits. Each dummy says what 1 means. The question wording is quoted, so the reader can judge what was really measured. The continuous variable states its unit.

Examiner: “Distance to centre” is the respondent's estimate, not a measured distance. Good operationalisation tables make that visible, and so does the limitations section.

Naming and coding rules that save you later

Short, lowercase names
finlit_score, not Financial Literacy Score (Q20-Q29). Put the long description in the variable label.
Name dummies after the category coded 1
female (1 = female), never gender. Then the coefficient reads directly as “female versus male”.
One coding per concept
If agreement items run 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, keep that direction everywhere and reverse-code negatively worded items before building scales.
Composites say how they are built
“PU = mean of PU1–PU4 (1–5)”, plus the reliability evidence (E4).
Transformations are visible
ln_allow = natural log of monthly allowance; state why (skewness, elasticity interpretation).
Missing values are documented
Which codes (−9, 999) meant missing, and what you did with them (C5).

Key takeaways

  1. Every variable needs a name, definition, source item, level, coding and expected role.
  2. Quote the question wording; say what 1 means; state units.
  3. The names in the codebook, the dataset and the regression tables must be identical.

How it reads in the thesis

Worksheet B3

  1. Operationalise “household vulnerability to floods” into two different variables, one binary and one continuous. Give the questionnaire item and the coding for each. (4 marks)

    Binary: flooded_before: “Has your home been flooded in the past ten years?” 1 = yes, 0 = no. Continuous: water_depth: “During the December 2014 flood, how deep was the water inside your home, in centimetres?” recorded in cm (ratio). Other good answers include distance to the river in km, or the number of flood events experienced (a count).

  2. Find four problems in this codebook row: “Gender | Gender of respondent | Scale | 1, 2 | Positive”. (4 marks)

    (1) The name should be a dummy named after the category coded 1, such as female. (2) “Scale” is not a measurement level; gender is nominal (binary). (3) The coding “1, 2” does not say which value is which, and should be 0/1 for a dummy. (4) “Positive” is meaningless without saying which category the sign refers to and why; give a source or call it a control.

  3. Your PU scale has four items, and item PU3 is negatively worded (“E-wallets make paying more complicated”). What must you do before computing PU, and how would you record it in the codebook? (3 marks)

    Reverse-code PU3 so that higher values mean higher usefulness: PU3r = 6 − PU3 on a 1–5 scale. Then compute PU as the mean of PU1, PU2, PU3r and PU4. Record in the codebook “PU3 reverse-coded (6 − PU3) before averaging”, and check that PU3r correlates positively with the other items.

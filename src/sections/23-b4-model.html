B43.4 Model specification

Section 3.4 states the econometric model that answers each research question: the estimating equation with every symbol defined, the estimator and why it suits the outcome and the identification problem, how inference is done, and how robustness will be checked. If your method is unusual, this is where you review the methodological literature that justifies it.

  • About 40 minutes
  • Equation templates for 10 models
  • Stata and Python estimation

After this section you can:

  1. write an estimating equation with correct subscripts, error term and defined symbols;
  2. choose an estimator from the outcome's measurement level, the data structure and the identification problem;
  3. write a methodological justification for a less familiar estimator.

What Section 3.4 contains

  1. The estimating equation(s), numbered (3.1), (3.2)…, one per research question or model. Use subscripts that match the data structure: \(i\) for units, \(t\) for time, \(it\) for panels (C2).
  2. A definition for every symbol: outcome, regressors (pointing to the codebook in 3.3), parameters, the error term, fixed effects.
  3. The estimator and its justification: why this estimator suits the outcome variable and your identification problem.
  4. The key assumptions and the diagnostic tests you will run.
  5. Inference: robust, clustered or bootstrapped standard errors, and why.
  6. What will be reported: coefficients, odds ratios, marginal effects, elasticities.
  7. Robustness checks: alternative estimators, samples, variable definitions.
  8. A short methodological review when the method is less standard (semi-nonparametric, partial proportional odds, fuzzy RD, spatial models), citing methods papers rather than textbook definitions.

Equation templates

Copy the form that matches your design, and replace the generic \(x\) with your variables.

Cross-section, continuous outcome (OLS)

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \beta_2 x_{2i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki} + u_i \tag{3.1}$$

Binary outcome (logit)

$$\Pr(y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \Lambda(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta) = \dfrac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta)}{1 + \exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta)} \tag{3.2}$$

Ordinal outcome (ordered logit)

$$y_i^* = \mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta + \varepsilon_i, \qquad y_i = j \text{ if } \kappa_{j-1} < y_i^* \le \kappa_j,\; j = 1,\dots,J \tag{3.3}$$

In the partial proportional odds model (Williams, 2006), some coefficients are allowed to differ across the cut-points \(j\): \(\Pr(y_i > j) = \Lambda(\alpha_j + \mathbf{x}_{1i}'\boldsymbol\beta_1 + \mathbf{x}_{2i}'\boldsymbol\beta_{2j})\).

Nominal outcome (multinomial logit)

$$\Pr(y_i = m \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \dfrac{\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta_m)}{\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{M}\exp(\mathbf{x}_i'\boldsymbol\beta_h)}, \quad \boldsymbol\beta_1 = \mathbf{0} \tag{3.4}$$

Time series (one unit, many periods)

$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_t + \beta_2 y_{t-1} + u_t \tag{3.5}$$

Pooled cross-sections, difference-in-differences

$$y_{it} = \beta_0 + \delta_0\, Post_t + \beta_1\, Treat_i + \delta_1 (Post_t \times Treat_i) + \mathbf{x}_{it}'\boldsymbol\gamma + u_{it} \tag{3.6}$$

Panel, fixed effects

$$y_{it} = \mathbf{x}_{it}'\boldsymbol\beta + a_i + \lambda_t + u_{it} \tag{3.7}$$

Instrumental variables (two-stage least squares)

$$x_i = \pi_0 + \pi_1 z_i + \mathbf{w}_i'\boldsymbol\pi + v_i, \qquad y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \hat x_i + \mathbf{w}_i'\boldsymbol\gamma + u_i \tag{3.8}$$

Regression discontinuity

$$y_i = \alpha + \tau D_i + f(r_i - c) + D_i\, g(r_i - c) + u_i, \quad D_i = \mathbf{1}[r_i \ge c] \tag{3.9}$$

In a fuzzy RD, crossing the cut-off \(c\) raises the probability of treatment without determining it, and \(D_i\) instruments actual treatment, giving a first stage, a reduced form and a second stage (see B2, Example 3).

Examiner: Why is there no error term in Equation (3.4)? Because a probability model has none. Know which of your equations should have one.

Inference: which standard errors?

Heteroskedasticity-robust
The default for cross-sections (vce(robust) in Stata; cov_type="HC1" in Python).
Clustered
When observations are grouped (students in classes, households in villages, years within a state). Cluster at the level of treatment assignment or sampling (vce(cluster id)). With fewer than about 30 clusters, say how you deal with it (for example, a wild cluster bootstrap).
Newey–West (HAC)
For time series with serial correlation (newey).
Survey design
With complex survey data (strata, clusters, weights), use the design (svyset then svy:).

When the method needs a methodological review

A standard estimator needs only a sentence and a textbook citation. A less familiar one needs a short review of the methods literature: what problem the method solves, why the usual alternative fails here, and evidence that the method works. Two examples:

  • Partial proportional odds. The ordered logit assumes each regressor's effect is the same at every cut-point (parallel lines). When a Brant test rejects this for some regressors, Williams (2006) shows how to relax it only for those, keeping the model parsimonious.
  • Semi-nonparametric binary choice. Logit and probit fix the error distribution. Gallant and Nychka (1987) proposed approximating an unknown density with a Hermite series, so the error distribution is estimated rather than assumed. Soon et al. (2018) use this approach for flood evacuation decisions.

Key takeaways

  1. Number every equation, define every symbol, and match subscripts to the data structure.
  2. The outcome's measurement level chooses the family of model; the identification problem chooses the design.
  3. State the standard errors and what you will report; review the methods literature for non-standard estimators.

References cited

  • Gallant, A. R., & Nychka, D. W. (1987). Semi-nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation. Econometrica, 55(2), 363–390.
  • Soon, J.-J., Kamaruddin, R., & Anuar, A. R. (2018). Flood victims' evacuation decisions: A semi-nonparametric estimation. International Journal of Emergency Services, 7(2), 134–146.
  • Williams, R. (2006). Generalized ordered logit/partial proportional odds models for ordinal dependent variables. The Stata Journal, 6(1), 58–82.

Worked example: three outcomes, three models

The e-wallet survey (400 students, hypothetical) has three outcomes at three measurement levels, so Section 3.4 specifies three models. Here they are estimated on the clean data, using 06_model_specification.do and its notebook.

Reading the output

  • The LPM coefficient on PU (0.128) and the logit average marginal effect (0.119) tell the same story: one more point of perceived usefulness goes with a 12–13 percentage-point higher probability of using an e-wallet. When the two agree, the result does not hinge on the functional form.
  • The logit coefficient (0.874) is a change in log-odds; \(e^{0.874} = 2.40\) is the odds ratio.
  • Financial literacy has no detectable association with use once perceptions are controlled for. That is a substantive finding for Chapter 4, so the model is not “wrong”.

The ordered logit's effect of PU on the probability of answering “strongly agree” is 0.162: one more point of PU goes with a 16-point higher chance of the top category.

Worksheet B4

  1. Write the estimating equation, with subscripts, for: household monthly saving (RM) regressed on financial literacy, income, age and number of children, using a 2024 household survey. Then rewrite it for a panel of the same households observed in 2020, 2022 and 2024 with household fixed effects. (6 marks)

    Cross-section: \(saving_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 finlit_i + \beta_2 income_i + \beta_3 age_i + \beta_4 children_i + u_i\).

    Panel: \(saving_{it} = \beta_1 finlit_{it} + \beta_2 income_{it} + \beta_3 children_{it} + a_i + \lambda_t + u_{it}\), \(t \in \{2020, 2022, 2024\}\). Age drops out (or is collinear with \(\lambda_t\) and \(a_i\)) because everyone ages two years between waves. The intercept is absorbed by the fixed effects.

  2. A student plans to estimate “the number of times a household used BNM's online banking in the past month” with OLS. Comment on the choice and suggest an alternative. (4 marks)

    The outcome is a non-negative count, probably with many zeros and a long right tail. OLS can predict negative counts and gives poor inference here. Poisson regression with robust standard errors is the natural choice (consistent if the mean is correctly specified); a negative binomial model handles overdispersion. Report incidence-rate ratios or marginal effects. OLS can still be reported as a robustness check.

  3. Write a three-sentence methodological justification for using clustered standard errors in a study of 1,200 students sampled by randomly selecting 40 tutorial classes. (3 marks)

    “Students were sampled in clusters (tutorial classes), and students in the same class share instructors and peer influences, so their errors are likely to be correlated. Ignoring this correlation would understate standard errors and overstate significance. Standard errors are therefore clustered at the class level, the level at which sampling took place; with 40 clusters, conventional cluster-robust inference is adequate.”

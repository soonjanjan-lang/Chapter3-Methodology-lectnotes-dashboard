B53.5 Ethics, limitations and putting the chapter together

The last section closes the chapter: research ethics, the limits of the design, and a short summary that hands over to the results. This page also gives the whole chapter's skeleton, a checklist to run before you submit, and the queries examiners raise most often.

Writing the ethics section

  • Approval: the committee and the reference number, for example “approved by the university's Research Ethics Committee (ref. …)”.
  • Informed consent: how participants were told the purpose, that taking part was voluntary, and that they could withdraw; how consent was recorded.
  • Confidentiality and anonymity: whether names were collected, how identifiers were separated from answers.
  • Data protection: storage, access, retention period; in Malaysia, handling personal data in line with the Personal Data Protection Act 2010.
  • Vulnerable participants and sensitive topics: extra safeguards, for example for flood victims, minors or low-income households.
  • Secondary data: the licence or terms of use, and any restricted-access agreement.

Writing limitations without undermining the thesis

State limitations of design, not of effort. Name each limitation, its likely consequence, and what you did about it.

The queries examiners raise most often

“Why this sample size?”
Answer with a precision or power calculation (D4).
“Is the sample representative?”
Compare the sample's profile with the population on known characteristics; be honest about non-probability recruitment (D3).
“Where did these items come from?”
The adoption and adaptation table (E2).
“Is a Likert item interval data?”
Explain how you treat single items and composite scales (E3).
“Alpha is 0.95; is that good?”
Possibly redundant items; report omega and validity evidence too (E4).
“Is this effect causal?”
State the identifying assumption, or reword as an association (A2).

Model write-ups: ethics, limitations, summary

Worksheet B5

  1. Rewrite this limitation so that it helps rather than hurts the thesis: “Due to time constraints, the sample size was small and the results may not be accurate.” (4 marks)

    “With 150 respondents, the study has 80% power to detect medium associations (f² = 0.15 with five predictors needs 92 observations) but not small ones, so non-significant estimates for weaker predictors should not be read as evidence of no association. Confidence intervals are reported so readers can judge precision.” It names the consequence, quantifies it and says what was done.

  2. Run the 15-item checklist on a draft Chapter 3 (your own or a classmate's). Which three items failed? Write one sentence for each saying how you will fix it. (6 marks)

    Answers vary. The most common failures in drafts are item 4 (sample size justified only by Krejcie–Morgan or by “rule of thumb”), item 7 (reliability quoted from the original study rather than the student's own sample) and item 13 (causal verbs such as “impact” with a cross-sectional design). Fixes: add a power calculation; report alpha, CR and AVE from your data; reword to “associated with”.

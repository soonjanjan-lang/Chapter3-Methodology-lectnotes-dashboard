C3Collecting primary data: surveys, interviews and focus groups

Primary data are collected by you, for your research questions. The three workhorses are the questionnaire survey, the interview and the focus group discussion. Each answers a different kind of question, and each has to be described in Chapter 3 in its own way.

After this section you can:

  1. choose between a survey, interviews and FGDs, and justify the choice;
  2. choose a survey mode and anticipate its biases;
  3. describe fieldwork in Chapter 3 so an examiner can judge its quality.

Many economics theses combine them. Interviews or FGDs before a survey help to write better items. Interviews after a survey help to explain surprising patterns (an explanatory sequential design).

Protecting data quality in a survey

  • Pretest and pilot before fieldwork: cognitive interviews, then a pilot of 20–40 people outside the sample (E1).
  • Train enumerators and give them a field manual; check a sample of their questionnaires.
  • Build checks into online forms: valid ranges, required answers for key items, skip logic, an attention-check item.
  • Send reminders: two or three reminders typically raise response rates noticeably.
  • Record the paradata: start and finish times, mode, enumerator ID. They help you detect speeders and interviewer effects.

Interviews

Structured
The same questions in the same order; close to a spoken questionnaire.
Semi-structured
A guide of main questions and follow-up probes, with freedom to pursue what the participant raises. The usual choice in economics and business theses.
Unstructured
A conversation around a topic; used in exploratory and ethnographic work.

Kallio et al. (2016) give a five-phase framework for building a semi-structured interview guide. Record interviews (with consent), transcribe them, and analyse them systematically, for example with Braun and Clarke's (2006) thematic analysis. For the number of interviews, justify it by saturation (Guest et al., 2006) or by information power (Malterud et al., 2016), not by a round number.

Focus group discussions

An FGD is a moderated discussion among six to ten people who share a relevant characteristic, such as B40 mothers, SME owners in Kedah or first-time flood victims. The interaction is the point: participants react to each other. Run several groups so you are not relying on one group's dynamics, and keep groups homogeneous on the dimension that matters (Morgan, 1996).

Examiner: Did one FGD of eight people give you “the community's view”? Say how many groups, who was in them, and how you handled dominant speakers.

Key takeaways

  1. Surveys measure and count; interviews and FGDs explain.
  2. The survey mode shapes coverage, response and bias: report it and its consequences.
  3. For qualitative work, justify the number of interviews or groups and describe the analysis method.

Worked example: a mixed-methods flood study

Research problem: Why do some flood victims refuse to evacuate? A design that uses all three methods:

  1. Two FGDs (village heads; women who stayed at home during the last flood) to learn how evacuation decisions are discussed and which reasons come up: protecting property, distrust of warnings, conditions at relief centres.
  2. A face-to-face household survey of flood-affected households, sampled from district relief lists, measuring evacuation (yes/no), prior flood experience, notification, awareness programmes and distance to the evacuation centre, as in Soon et al. (2018).
  3. Twelve semi-structured interviews with households that did not evacuate despite being notified, to explain a pattern the survey cannot.

Chapter 3 would report each strand: its sample and how it was chosen, its instrument, its fieldwork, and how the strands connect. In this design, the FGDs inform the questionnaire items and the interviews explain survey results.

How it reads in the thesis

Worksheet C3

  1. For each aim, choose a survey, interviews or FGDs, and justify the choice in one sentence: (a) estimate the share of hawkers in Penang who accept DuitNow QR; (b) understand why older hawkers resist QR payments; (c) explore how a residents' association talks about a proposed toll. (6 marks)

    (a) Survey: a proportion needs a large, preferably probability sample. (b) Semi-structured interviews: reasons and experiences in the hawkers' own words, from a purposive sample of older hawkers. (c) FGD: the aim is the group's shared and contested views, which emerge through interaction.

  2. Your online survey of SME owners received 180 responses from 1,500 emails (12%). Give two ways the low response could bias your results, and two things you can report or do about it. (4 marks)

    Bias: owners who are more digitally active, or more interested in the topic, are more likely to reply, overstating digital adoption; very small or struggling firms may be under-represented. Responses: compare respondents with the frame on known characteristics (sector, size, state) and report the comparison; weight to the frame's composition; compare early and late responders as a rough check; state the limitation and restrict generalisation.

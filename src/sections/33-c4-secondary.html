C4Collecting secondary data: where to find it and how to report it

Secondary data were collected by someone else, usually a statistics agency, central bank or international organisation, for their own purposes. They are cheaper and often larger and better sampled than anything a PhD student can collect. The cost is that you must understand exactly how they were produced.

  • About 25 minutes
  • Directory of 20 verified sources
  • How to cite a dataset

After this section you can:

  1. find a suitable public dataset for an economics research question;
  2. evaluate a dataset's fitness for your question before you commit to it;
  3. cite and describe datasets so another researcher can retrieve the same data.

Before you commit: six questions about any dataset

  1. Who collected it, and why? An administrative register, a household survey and a model-based estimate have very different error properties.
  2. How was it collected? For surveys: the sampling design, sample size, mode and response rate. Most national household surveys need their weights, and often their strata and clusters, in your estimation.
  3. Does its definition match your concept? “Unemployment” in the WDI is a modelled ILO estimate; in DOSM's Labour Force Survey it is a survey estimate. They can differ.
  4. What is the coverage? Years, countries or states, gaps, and breaks in series when methods change.
  5. Which version? Datasets are revised. Penn World Table results can change between versions (Johnson et al., 2013). Record the version and the date you downloaded it.
  6. Can you use it? The licence, any registration, restricted microdata agreements, and the time they take to approve.
Examiner: Large surveys are not immune to error. Response rates and reporting quality in major household surveys have been falling (Meyer et al., 2015). Say what you know about your dataset's quality.

Citing a dataset

Treat a dataset like a publication: author (the organisation), year, title, version, “[Data set]”, publisher and URL, with the access date for data that change.

World Bank. (2026). World Development Indicators [Data set]. https://databank.worldbank.org/source/world-development-indicators (accessed 14 January 2026).

Penn World Table: cite the version you used and its documentation paper (Feenstra et al., 2015, for version 8 onwards), and record the version number, for example PWT 10.01 or 11.0.

Combining datasets

Theses often merge sources, such as WDI controls with PWT productivity. Merge on standard identifiers (ISO 3-letter country codes and year; DOSM state codes), check how many observations matched, and report the final sample.

use wdi_extract.dta, clear // iso3 year inflation unemp growth merge 1:1 iso3 year using pwt_extract.dta // iso3 year rgdpna ctfp tabulate _merge // 3 = matched in both keep if _merge == 3 drop _merge wdi = pd.read_csv("wdi_extract.csv") # iso3, year, inflation, unemp, growth pwt = pd.read_csv("pwt_extract.csv") # iso3, year, rgdpna, ctfp m = wdi.merge(pwt, on=["iso3", "year"], how="outer", indicator=True, validate="one_to_one") print(m["_merge"].value_counts()) # both / left_only / right_only panel = m[m["_merge"] == "both"].drop(columns="_merge")
Examiner: How many country-years were lost in the merge, and are the missing ones random? Report it.

Key takeaways

  1. Know who collected the data, how, and whether the definitions match your concepts.
  2. Record version and access date; cite datasets like publications.
  3. Report merges and the observations they cost.

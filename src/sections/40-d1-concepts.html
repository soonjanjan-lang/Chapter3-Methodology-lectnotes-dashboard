D1Population, sampling frame and sample

Before choosing a sampling design you must be precise about whom your findings describe, which list you can actually draw from, and the units you select. Most sampling problems that examiners find start here, not in the formula.

  • About 15 minutes
  • Worksheet with model answers

After this section you can:

  1. distinguish population, target population, sampling frame, sampling unit and sample;
  2. identify coverage problems in a sampling frame;
  3. separate sampling error from non-sampling error.

From population to sample

Population
The full set of units the research question concerns.
Target population
The population defined precisely by who, where and when. Your conclusions apply here.
Sampling frame
A list or procedure that identifies the units you can select: enrolment records, the SSM company register, DOSM enumeration blocks, a membership list.
Element (unit of analysis)
The unit you measure and analyse: a student, a household, a firm.
Sampling unit
The unit you select. It can differ from the element: you may select villages, then households within them.
Sample
The units selected. The achieved sample is the subset who actually respond.
Census
Observing every unit in the target population, which is sensible when it is small (for example, every licensed commercial bank).

Frame problems

  • Undercoverage: units missing from the frame. An online panel misses people without internet; a telephone directory misses mobile-only households.
  • Overcoverage: ineligible units on the frame, such as closed firms still registered, or graduates still on an alumni list.
  • Duplicates: units listed twice have twice the chance of selection.
  • Clustering: one frame entry covers several elements, such as one address with several households.
Examiner: “The population is all SMEs in Malaysia.” Which list did you sample from, and who is not on it?

Two kinds of error

Table D1.1. Sampling and non-sampling error
ErrorCauseShrinks with a larger sample?What helps
Sampling errorChance: observing a sample, not everyoneYes, in proportion to \(1/\sqrt{n}\)Larger probability samples; stratification
Coverage errorThe frame misses or adds unitsNoA better frame; weighting to known totals
Non-response errorThose who respond differ from those who do notNoReminders, incentives; non-response analysis (Groves, 2006)
Measurement errorQuestions measure poorly; recall; social desirabilityNoGood questionnaire design and pretesting (E1)

A large sample cures only the first row. A survey of 5,000 self-selected respondents can be more biased than a well-run random sample of 400.

Key takeaways

  1. Define the target population by who, where and when; name the frame and its gaps.
  2. The sampling unit and the unit of analysis can differ.
  3. Only sampling error falls as n rises; coverage, non-response and measurement error do not.

References cited

  • Groves, R. M. (2006). Nonresponse rates and nonresponse bias in household surveys. Public Opinion Quarterly, 70(5), 646–675.

Worksheet D1

  1. For the study “Digital payment adoption among hawkers in George Town”, define the target population, a feasible sampling frame, the sampling unit and the unit of analysis. Identify one coverage problem with your frame. (5 marks)

    Target population: licensed hawkers operating within the George Town municipal area in 2026. Frame: the city council's hawker licence register. Sampling unit: the licensed stall (from the register). Unit of analysis: the hawker (stall operator). Coverage problem: unlicensed hawkers are missing (undercoverage), and licences held for stalls no longer trading are included (overcoverage). Describe both and check eligibility at the start of each interview.

  2. A student argues: “My online survey has 2,000 responses, so sampling error is tiny and my results represent all Malaysian youth.” Evaluate the claim. (4 marks)

    A large n reduces sampling error only for a probability sample, where sampling error is defined. A self-selected online sample has undercoverage (youth without internet or outside the sharing networks) and self-selection bias, neither of which falls with n. The results describe the respondents and perhaps similar young people, not all Malaysian youth. Comparing the sample with national demographics and weighting can help, but cannot remove bias on unmeasured characteristics.

D2Probability sampling: simple random, systematic, stratified

In a probability sample every member of the target population has a known, non-zero chance of selection. That known chance is what allows formal statistical inference: sampling distributions, standard errors and confidence intervals all assume random selection.

1. Simple random sampling (SRS)

Every unit has an equal probability of selection, \(n/N\), and every possible sample of size \(n\) is equally likely. Draw it with a random number generator applied to the sampling frame, or, for a small frame, by drawing paper slips out of a bowl.

  • Needs: a complete list of the population.
  • Strengths: simple; unbiased; the textbook formulas for standard errors apply directly.
  • Weaknesses: small subgroups may be under-represented by chance; can be costly when units are spread out.

2. Systematic random sampling

Select every \(k\)-th unit from the frame after a random start between 1 and \(k\), where \(k = N/n\) (rounded down). With \(N = 2{,}400\) and \(n = 331\), \(k = 7\); a random start of 2 selects units 2, 9, 16, 23 and so on.

  • Strengths: easy in the field, such as every 7th customer leaving a shop or every 10th house on a street; spreads the sample evenly through the list.
  • Weakness: vulnerable to bias when the list has a pattern that matches \(k\), for example if every 10th house on a street is a corner house, or every 7th day is a Sunday.
Examiner: Report the interval and the random start, and say how the frame was ordered.

3. Stratified random sampling

Partition the population into non-overlapping strata (for example urban/rural, ethnic group, industry, faculty) and draw a separate random sample within each. Stratify on characteristics that are related to your outcome, because that is what improves precision.

Proportionate allocation
Each stratum's sample size is proportional to its population size: \(n_h = n \times N_h / N\). Every unit has the same selection probability, so no weights are needed.
Disproportionate allocation
Small strata are over-sampled to improve precision for subgroup estimates. Selection probabilities differ, so population estimates need design weights \(w_h = N_h / n_h\).

4. Cluster and multistage sampling

When there is no list of individuals, or they are spread over a wide area, select groups first (villages, schools, enumeration blocks) and then units within them. National household surveys typically use multistage stratified designs, selecting areas first and then living quarters within them. Cluster samples are cheaper per interview but less precise than an SRS of the same size, because units in a cluster resemble each other. The design effect quantifies the loss (D4).

Key takeaways

  1. Probability sampling means known, non-zero selection probabilities, which makes inference possible.
  2. Stratify on variables related to the outcome; allocate disproportionately when subgroups matter, then weight.
  3. Report the frame, design, allocation, random seed or start, and weights.

How it reads in the thesis

Worksheet D2

  1. A frame lists 6,000 households: 3,600 urban and 2,400 rural. You need n = 361. (a) Give the proportionate allocation. (b) You want equal precision for urban and rural estimates: give an allocation and the design weights. (6 marks)

    (a) Urban: 361 × 3,600/6,000 = 216.6, so 217; rural: 361 × 2,400/6,000 = 144.4, so 144 (total 361). (b) Equal allocation: 181 urban and 180 rural. Weights: urban 3,600/181 = 19.89; rural 2,400/180 = 13.33. Each sampled urban household represents about 20 households and each rural household about 13.

  2. You plan to survey every 8th customer leaving a supermarket between 9 a.m. and 9 p.m. on weekdays. Is this systematic sampling? What could bias it? (4 marks)

    It is systematic selection from a flow of customers, which works well in the field, but the population covered is only weekday shoppers at that supermarket. Biases: weekend shoppers and working people who shop in the evening or at weekends are under-represented; groups arriving together may all be skipped or all selected. It is a probability sample of customer visits on surveyed days, not of households. Choose a random start, sample different days and times, and state the target population precisely.

  3. Use the simulator: draw ten proportionate stratified samples and ten simple random samples of n = 20. How does the number of agriculture firms vary across draws under each design, and why? (3 marks)

    Proportionate stratified sampling always gives 3 agriculture firms (20 × 30/200). SRS gives a varying number, typically 1 to 6, sometimes 0, because the stratum count is left to chance. Stratification fixes each stratum's share and so removes this source of sampling variation.

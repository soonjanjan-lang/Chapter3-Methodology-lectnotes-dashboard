D3Non-probability sampling: convenience, purposive, snowball

In non-probability sampling, selection probabilities are unknown, or zero for some units. These designs are used when no sampling frame exists, when the population is hidden, or when the aim is depth rather than statistical generalisation. Findings are hard to generalise, and Chapter 3 must say so honestly.

  • About 20 minutes
  • Four designs with economics examples

After this section you can:

  1. explain convenience, purposive, snowball and quota sampling;
  2. judge when a non-probability design is defensible;
  3. describe one in Chapter 3 without over-claiming.

1. Convenience sampling

Recruiting whoever is available and willing: students in your class, shoppers at a mall, people who click a link shared on social media.

  • Use it for: pilots, pretests, exploratory studies, testing an instrument.
  • Risk: volunteers and the easily reached differ systematically from the rest. A bigger convenience sample is a bigger biased sample.

2. Purposive (judgemental) sampling

Deliberately selecting units that meet specific criteria, because they hold the information the study needs: female CEOs, judges, central bank economists, SME owners who stopped accepting cash, households flooded three times. Palinkas et al. (2015) describe variants:

  • Expert sampling: people with specialist knowledge.
  • Maximum variation: cases that differ widely, to capture the range of experience.
  • Typical case: cases that represent the usual situation.
  • Critical or extreme case: cases where a phenomenon is most visible.

Purposive sampling is the standard choice for interviews and case studies. State the selection criteria precisely so readers can judge them.

3. Snowball sampling

Existing participants recruit further participants from their networks. It is essential for stigmatised, hidden or illegal populations with no list: undocumented migrant workers, borrowers from unlicensed moneylenders, drug users, the homeless (Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981).

  • Risk: samples reflect the starting “seeds” and their networks; well-connected people are over-represented.
  • Improvement: start from several diverse seeds, limit referrals per person, and record who referred whom. Respondent-driven sampling formalises this to allow some population estimates (Heckathorn, 1997).

4. Quota sampling

Fill quotas that match known population proportions, for example 50% women, or ethnic shares from the census, but select people within each quota by convenience. The sample looks representative on the quota variables, yet selection within quotas is not random. Quota sampling is common in market research and opinion polling.

Examiner: You matched the census on gender and ethnicity. Does that make the sample representative of attitudes to e-wallets? Only on those two variables, not on anything else.

When is a non-probability design defensible?

Table D3.1. Matching the design to the purpose
PurposeDefensible?What to write
Depth: why and how (interviews, FGDs)Yes, usually purposiveSelection criteria; how many and why (saturation or information power)
Hidden population with no frameYes, snowball or respondent-drivenSeeds, referral chains, limits on generalisation
Testing relationships (theory testing)SometimesArgue why the relationship should hold beyond the sample; compare the sample's profile with the population
Estimating a population proportion or meanWeaklyAvoid design-based margins of error; any model-based adjustment needs stated assumptions (Baker et al., 2013)

Conventional margins of sampling error do not apply to non-probability samples (Baker et al., 2013). Do not report “±5% at 95% confidence” for a convenience sample, and do not cite Krejcie and Morgan as if it made the sample representative (D4).

Key takeaways

  1. Convenience for pilots; purposive for depth; snowball for hidden populations; quota for a surface match.
  2. Selection probabilities are unknown, so statistical generalisation is limited.
  3. Describe the recruitment honestly and restrict your claims.

Worked examples in economics

Convenience
Mall-intercept survey of e-wallet use. 300 shoppers approached at a Penang mall over two weekends. Fast and cheap, but it over-represents urban, mobile, higher-income shoppers. Suitable for piloting the questionnaire, not for estimating national adoption.
Purposive
Interviews with SME owners who stopped accepting cash. Twelve owners chosen because they made the switch, varied deliberately by sector and firm size (maximum variation). The study explains why firms switch, not how many do.
Snowball
Borrowers from unlicensed moneylenders (Ah Long). No list exists and the topic is sensitive. Start from five seeds introduced by community organisations in different districts; each participant may refer up to three others; record the referral chains. Report results as describing these networks.
Quota
Attitudes to the sales and services tax. 400 adults recruited online with quotas matching the census on gender, ethnicity and urban/rural residence. The sample matches on those variables only; attitudes may still differ from the population's.

How it reads in the thesis

Worksheet D3

  1. Choose a non-probability design for each, and write the selection criteria: (a) understanding how Rohingya refugees in Malaysia earn a living; (b) how bank economists form inflation expectations; (c) piloting a questionnaire on household debt. (6 marks)

    (a) Snowball from several community-organisation seeds in different states; criteria: adult refugees who have earned income in Malaysia in the past year. (b) Purposive (expert) sampling; criteria: economists in research or treasury roles at licensed banks with at least three years' experience forecasting. (c) Convenience; criteria: adults with at least one household loan, recruited from the researcher's networks, outside the main sample.

  2. Find the problems in this sentence: “A total of 384 respondents were selected using convenience sampling, as recommended by Krejcie and Morgan (1970), ensuring the sample is representative of the population with a 5% margin of error.” (4 marks)

    (1) Krejcie and Morgan give a sample size for a probability sample; they do not recommend convenience sampling. (2) A convenience sample cannot be called representative because of its size. (3) A design-based margin of error does not apply to a non-probability sample. (4) 384 respondents were not “selected”; they volunteered, so describe recruitment accurately. Rewrite to describe the recruitment and justify n by the analysis (power), with an honest statement of limits.

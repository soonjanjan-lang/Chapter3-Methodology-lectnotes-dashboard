E2Adopting and adapting established questionnaires

You rarely need to write every item from scratch. Established instruments come with evidence of validity and reliability, and they let you compare your results with earlier studies. Using them well means choosing carefully, changing as little as possible, documenting every change, and testing again in your own sample.

  • About 25 minutes
  • Adaptation log template
  • Worked example from TAM

After this section you can:

  1. distinguish adopting, adapting and developing an instrument;
  2. evaluate an existing instrument's fit for your study;
  3. document adaptations so an examiner can see exactly what changed and why.

Adopt, adapt or develop?

Adopt
Use the instrument as published: same items, same response format, same scoring. Its validity evidence carries over most directly, although you still check reliability in your sample.
Adapt
Modify it: change the context words, tense or target behaviour; translate it; drop or add items; change the response format. Every change weakens the inherited evidence, so validate again.
Develop
Write new items when no suitable instrument exists. This needs the full scale-development process: item generation, expert review, pretesting and factor analysis (Hinkin, 1998; Boateng et al., 2018).

Map each research question to an instrument

Start from the research questions and find an established instrument for each construct, recording its source, length and response format. Here is an example from a study of a teacher preparation programme and co-teaching:

Table E2.1. Research questions and established questionnaire instruments
Research questionEstablished questionnaire instrument
RQ1: Teacher efficacy = f(TPP programme)Teachers' Sense of Efficacy Scale (TSES); Tschannen-Moran & Hoy (2001); 24 items; 9-point rating scale, from 1 (Nothing much I can do) to 9 (I can do a great deal)
RQ2: Student non-academic outcome = f(co-teaching): leadership, behaviour and motivation = f(co-teaching)Student Leadership Practices Inventory (SLPI); Kouzes & Posner (2005); 30 items; 5-point rating scale, from 1 (Rarely/Never) to 5 (Very frequently)
Motivated Self-Directed Learning & Collaborative Learning (MSDLCL); Choy et al. (2016); 28 items; 7-point rating scale, from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 7 (Strongly agree)
RQ3: Pedagogical skills = f(co-teaching)Framework for Teaching (FFT); Danielson (1996); 22 items; 4-point rating scale, from 1 (Unsatisfactory) to 4 (Distinguished)

Notice that the four instruments use 9-, 5-, 7- and 4-point formats. Keep each instrument's original format unless you have a strong reason: the published validity and reliability evidence was obtained with that format. Different formats in different sections are fine if the instructions are clear.

Examiner: If you converted a 9-point scale to 5 points “for consistency”, you adapted the instrument. Say so, and show its reliability and validity in your data.

A procedure for adopting and adapting

  1. Define the construct precisely before searching. “Financial literacy” as objective knowledge needs a test; as confidence it needs attitude items.
  2. Search the literature on your construct: the original development papers, review articles, highly cited applications, measurement databases.
  3. Evaluate each candidate: does its definition match yours? What validity and reliability evidence exists, and in which populations? What length and format? Is it free to use?
  4. Get permission where needed. Some instruments are copyrighted or sold under licence; write to the authors or publisher and keep their reply for your appendix.
  5. Adapt minimally and systematically: change only what the new context requires, keep the response format, and record every change in an adaptation log.
  6. Translate properly: forward translation, independent back-translation, reconciliation (Brislin, 1970; Beaton et al., 2000).
  7. Review and pretest: expert content review (content validity index), cognitive interviews, pilot (E1, E4).
  8. Re-establish evidence in your sample: reliability (alpha, omega), factor structure, convergent and discriminant validity. A scale's reliability in someone else's sample is not evidence about yours.
  9. Report: a sources table in Section 3.1 and the full adaptation log in an appendix.
Examiner: “The questionnaire is reliable because it was adopted from a published article.” Reliability belongs to scores in a sample, not to a printed questionnaire. Show me yours.

Key takeaways

  1. Prefer established instruments; adopt where you can, adapt where you must, develop only when nothing fits.
  2. Change as little as possible, keep the response format, and log every change.
  3. Re-establish reliability and validity in your own sample.

Worked example: adapting perceived usefulness from Davis (1989)

Davis's perceived-usefulness items were written for workers using a computer system at work. The e-wallet study needs them for students paying with a phone. The adaptation log records each decision.

Table E2.2. Adaptation log for perceived usefulness
Original item (Davis, 1989; [system] stands for the technology studied)Adapted itemChangeReason
Using [system] in my job would enable me to accomplish tasks more quickly.Using an e-wallet enables me to complete payments more quickly.Context (job → payments); tense (would → present)Respondents are current users making personal payments
Using [system] in my job would increase my productivity.Using an e-wallet makes managing my daily payments more efficient.Context; “productivity” → “efficient”“Productivity” has no clear meaning for personal payments (cognitive interviews)
Using [system] would enhance my effectiveness on the job.Using an e-wallet improves the convenience of my payments.Context; target outcomeExpert panel judged “convenience” the relevant benefit
I would find [system] useful in my job.I find e-wallets useful for my everyday payments.Context; tenseAs above
Using [system] would improve my job performance.(dropped)Item removedNo meaningful equivalent for students; content coverage checked by expert review

Response format kept as a five-point agreement scale. The Malay version was back-translated. In the pilot (n = 30), the adapted four-item scale had α = 0.84. In the main survey, α = 0.86 and CR = 0.86 (E4).

Worksheet E2

  1. You want to measure “trust in Islamic banks” among Malaysian depositors. Describe how you would decide whether to adopt, adapt or develop an instrument. (5 marks)

    Define the construct (trust in the integrity, competence and Shariah compliance of Islamic banks). Search the literature for validated bank-trust scales, especially any used with Islamic banking customers. Evaluate the fit of definition, validity evidence, population and language. If a validated scale fits closely and was used with Malaysian or similar depositors, adopt it. If it measures trust in conventional banks, adapt the items (adding Shariah-compliance content), get permission, back-translate, run an expert review and a pilot, and re-validate. Develop new items only if no scale covers the construct.

  2. A student changes a validated 7-point scale to 5 points “to keep the questionnaire consistent” and cites the original alpha of 0.91 as evidence of reliability. Comment. (4 marks)

    Changing the response format is an adaptation, so the original evidence no longer transfers directly. The 0.91 was obtained in another sample with another format. Reliability must be estimated in the current sample. Keeping the original 7-point format is usually better; if changed, justify it, pilot the scale and report alpha (and omega) and factor structure from the current data.

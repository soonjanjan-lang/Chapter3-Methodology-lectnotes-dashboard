E3Likert scales and rating scales

The first misconception to correct: Likert scales and rating scales are not two mutually exclusive methods. A Likert-type item is one particular kind of rating scale. What matters is what you intend to measure, how respondents answer, and which analyses the answers can legitimately support.

  • Format examples
  • Which estimator for which outcome

After this section you can:

  1. distinguish a Likert-type item, a Likert scale and other rating scales;
  2. choose a response format to match each construct;
  3. choose an estimator for an outcome measured on these scales.

What is the difference?

A rating scale is the broad category: respondents evaluate something using ordered categories or numerical scores. A Likert-type item is a more specific format that asks for degree of agreement or disagreement with a statement. Strictly, a Likert scale is several related Likert-type items combined (summed or averaged) to measure one underlying construct (Likert, 1932). A single agreement question is a Likert-type item, although many researchers loosely call it a Likert scale.

Example A: Likert-type item

“Using ChatGPT improves my academic productivity.”

Measures agreement with a proposition.

Example B: numerical rating scale

“How satisfied are you with your university's Wi-Fi service?”

Very dissatisfied 7/10 Very satisfied

Directly evaluates satisfaction.

Both collect subjective evaluations, but the first operationalises agreement with a statement, while the second rates the thing itself.

A 1–10 rating is not automatically better than a five-point Likert item. More response categories do not by themselves produce interval-level measurement or improve validity (Sullivan & Artino, 2013; Norman, 2010).

When to use each

Agreement items for latent constructs. To measure perceived usefulness of AI, use several statements on the same five-point agreement format:

Table E3.2. A four-item perceived-usefulness scale
CodeStatement
PU1Using generative AI improves the quality of my academic work.
PU2Using generative AI helps me complete assignments more efficiently.
PU3Using generative AI enhances my academic productivity.
PU4Using generative AI makes it easier to understand difficult academic concepts.

If the items represent a common construct and support a defensible composite, one index is the mean:

$$PU_i = \frac{PU1_i + PU2_i + PU3_i + PU4_i}{4}$$

Before combining items, assess dimensionality and reliability (E4), rather than assuming that four similar statements make a valid scale.

Direct ratings for specific evaluations. “Overall, how satisfied are you with generative AI tools for your academic work?” (very dissatisfied to very satisfied) is direct, quick, and avoids an extra agreement statement. Agreement formats also invite acquiescence bias, the tendency to agree whatever the content. An experiment by Revilla, Saris and Krosnick (2014) found that five categories performed better than seven or eleven for the agree–disagree scales they examined. That is evidence for that context, not a universal rule.

Examiner: Choose the format for the construct, construct by construct. One format for every question in the instrument is a habit, not a design decision.

Which estimator? It depends on the outcome, not the label

Your choice of econometric method should depend on the measurement properties of the dependent variable, not on whether the questionnaire calls it a Likert or a rating scale.

Table E3.3. Outcome, example and analytical options
Dependent variableExampleAppropriate options
One five-point agreement itemAgreement that AI improves productivityOrdered logit or probit
One five-point satisfaction ratingSatisfaction with university Wi-FiOrdered logit or probit
One 1–10 ratingPerceived AI usefulnessOrdered logit/probit; OLS with justification and robustness checks
Mean of several validated itemsAI-dependence indexOLS can be considered; latent-variable models are alternatives
BinaryUses ChatGPT at least weekly (yes/no)Logit, probit, linear probability model
Observed frequencyNumber of days using AI in a weekCount or binomial models

Is OLS acceptable for Likert data? The debate is long-running. Individual items are ordinal: moving from 1 to 2 need not equal moving from 4 to 5 (Jamieson, 2004). Norman (2010) shows that parametric methods are robust with such data, and Carifio and Perla (2008) distinguish single items from summated scales. Liddell and Kruschke (2018) show that treating ordinal outcomes as metric can produce wrong conclusions. A defensible rule: for a single five-category outcome, start with an ordered logit or probit. For a validated composite of several items, OLS is reasonable, ideally with a sensitivity check. For complex latent constructs, consider ordinal CFA or SEM.

Perceptions versus behaviour

Table E3.4. Three ways to ask about AI dependence
FormatItemWhat it measures
Agreement“I frequently rely on ChatGPT to complete my assignments.”Self-reported agreement with a statement about reliance
Frequency rating“During the past seven days, how often did you use ChatGPT for academic work?” (never / once / 2–3 times / 4–6 times / 7 or more)Recalled frequency in ordered categories
Behavioural count“On how many of the past seven days did you use ChatGPT for academic work?”A self-reported count from 0 to 7 (recall error remains)

For frequency of use, the second and third formats beat the first. But even daily use is not dependence: dependence needs its own definition and, probably, a validated multi-item measure. Choose the measurement format from the construct and research question first, then choose the estimator.

Key takeaways

  1. A Likert-type item is one kind of rating scale; a Likert scale combines several such items.
  2. Match the format to each construct: agreement for latent attitudes, direct ratings, frequencies or counts for evaluations and behaviour.
  3. The outcome's measurement properties, not its label, decide the estimator.

Worked example: choosing formats for an AI study

A thesis on students' use of generative AI measures eight constructs. Rather than one format throughout, each construct gets the format that fits it.

Table E3.5. Construct and suggested measurement
ConstructSuggested measurement
Attitude towards AIFive-point agreement items
Perceived usefulnessAgreement items or direct usefulness ratings
Trust in AIValidated multi-item measure
Satisfaction with AIDirect five-point satisfaction rating
Frequency of AI useExplicit frequency categories or counts
Time spent using AIHours or minutes over a defined recall period
Perceived importance of a policyDirect importance rating
AI dependenceValidated multi-item construct, supplemented by behaviour questions

Questionnaire quality depends on whether items capture the construct, not only on the response scale. Scale development needs content validation, pretesting, tests of dimensionality, reliability and construct validity. Cronbach's alpha alone does not establish validity (E4).

Worksheet E3

  1. For each outcome, name the estimator: (a) a single item “I intend to apply for a PTPTN loan” (1–5); (b) the mean of six validated financial-anxiety items (1–5); (c) “How many times did you use Touch 'n Go eWallet last week?” (3 marks)

    (a) Ordered logit or probit, with a test of the parallel-lines assumption. (b) OLS is reasonable for a validated composite, with an ordered-model or latent-variable check. (c) A count model (Poisson or negative binomial), or OLS if counts are spread widely, reported with robustness checks.

  2. Rewrite “I use e-wallets frequently” (strongly disagree to strongly agree) into a behavioural count item, and explain what is gained. (3 marks)

    “On how many of the past seven days did you pay with an e-wallet? (0–7)”. The count has a defined reference period, is less prone to acquiescence, is ratio-level, and means the same to every respondent, whereas “frequently” is interpreted differently by different people.

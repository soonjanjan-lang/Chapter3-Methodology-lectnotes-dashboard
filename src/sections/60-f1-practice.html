Integrated tutorial: from research questions to Chapter 3

The case

A PhD student plans a thesis on “Remittances and children's schooling in rural Sabah.” The student wants to know (1) how many households receive remittances and how much; (2) whether receiving remittances is associated with higher education spending per child; and (3) whether any association is stronger for daughters than for sons. There is no list of households, but the district office keeps a list of the 120 villages in two districts, with their approximate number of households.

Work through the six steps. Each has a model answer.

  1. Step 1: Classify the research questions as descriptive, correlational or causal, and say what wording Chapter 4 can use (A2).

    RQ1 is descriptive (proportions and means). RQ2 and RQ3 are correlational: one cross-sectional survey cannot rule out that families who value education both migrate and spend more. Wording: “associated with”. A causal claim would need, for example, variation in remittances caused by shocks in migrants' destinations.

  2. Step 2: Design the sample (D2, D4). Choose a design, explain how households are selected, and justify the size.

    Two-stage cluster sample: select 30 villages with probability proportional to size, list all households in each, then select 15 households per village by systematic sampling, giving 450 households. Size: for RQ2, detecting a small partial association (f² = 0.02) with 8 controls at 80% power needs about 400 observations; with an expected design effect of 1 + (15 − 1) × 0.05 = 1.7, the effective sample of 450 is about 265, so either more villages (about 45) or a stated acceptance of lower power. Inflate for 85% response.

  3. Step 3: Build the RQ–method table (B2).

    RQOutcome (level)Main variableMethod
    RQ1 Incidence and size of remittancesReceives (binary); amount RM (ratio)—Weighted proportions and means, design-based SEs
    RQ2 Remittances and education spendingEducation spending per child (ratio; zeros possible)Receives remittances; amountOLS on log(1 + spending) or a two-part model; SEs clustered by village
    RQ3 Daughters versus sonsSpending per child (child-level)Remittances × girlChild-level regression with household controls and an interaction; clustered SEs

  4. Step 4: Write three codebook rows (B3): the remittance indicator, education spending, and the girl dummy.

    remit: household received money from a member living elsewhere in the past 12 months; item C1; nominal (binary); 1 = yes, 0 = no. edu_spend: spending on fees, books, uniforms, transport and tuition for the child in the past school year, RM; items D3–D7 summed; ratio. girl: child is female; household roster; nominal (binary); 1 = girl, 0 = boy.

  5. Step 5: Specify the model for RQ3 with correct subscripts (B4, C2).

    \(spend_{ch} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 remit_h + \beta_2 girl_{ch} + \beta_3 (remit_h \times girl_{ch}) + \mathbf{x}_{ch}'\boldsymbol\gamma + \mathbf{w}_h'\boldsymbol\delta + u_{ch}\), for child \(c\) in household \(h\). \(\beta_3\) answers RQ3. Child controls \(\mathbf{x}\): age, school level. Household controls \(\mathbf{w}\): income, parents' education, distance to school. Standard errors clustered by village (the primary sampling unit).

  6. Step 6: Name two limitations and their consequences (B5).

    (1) Cross-sectional data: remittance-receiving households may differ in unobserved ways (motivation, networks), so estimates are associations. (2) Recall of a year's education spending is error-prone; classical error in the outcome lowers precision but does not bias the coefficients, while systematic under-reporting by some groups would. Also acceptable: the two districts cannot represent all of Sabah.
